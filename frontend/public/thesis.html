Abstract

This thesis presents zkCredentials, a system for issuing, managing, and verifying academic credentials on the zkSync Era blockchain. The core problem it addresses is a tension that existing blockchain credential systems have not resolved: anchoring a credential on a public ledger proves its authenticity, but also exposes the student's identity to anyone who queries the chain. zkCredentials addresses this in two distinct ways. First, raw credential data is never stored on-chain — only a SHA-256 hash of each credential document is anchored in the registry, so the credential contents themselves remain private. Second, the system provides a ZK Authentication flow: a user proves knowledge of a secret key corresponding to a registered commitment via a Groth16 zero-knowledge proof over the BN254 elliptic curve, without revealing the key. Fresh per-session nullifiers prevent proof replay and are unlinkable through nullifier values alone, though sessions remain linkable at the wallet level via msg.sender (see Section 7.6). This is distinct from a full ZK credential possession proof; the circuit authenticates the user, while credential validity is checked separately through registry lookups. Four UUPS upgradeable smart contracts handle institution registration, certificate issuance, employer access, and ZK-based session management. The frontend is a React 18 single-page application. Chapter 7 measures proof generation time across three device classes and on-chain gas costs on zkSync Sepolia, and argues each security property by reduction to established cryptographic hardness assumptions.

Chapter 1

Introduction

Academic credentials sit at an uncomfortable intersection of authenticity and privacy. A hash on a public ledger proves a degree is genuine, but also tells anyone who queries the chain who holds it and when it was issued. This chapter explains why that matters, what this thesis does about it, and how the rest of the document is organised.

1.1 Problem Statement and Motivation

Academic credentials are central to hiring, further study, and professional licensing. The verification of these credentials today relies on a fragile chain of institutional trust: a university issues a paper or PDF certificate, an employer sends an email or pays a third-party service to confirm it, and the university replies — often days later, sometimes for a fee. This process is slow, costly, and opaque. Fraudulent credentials circulate widely; the HireRight 2025 Global Benchmark Report found that more than three-quarters of employers globally uncovered candidate discrepancies in the previous 12 months, with education credential discrepancies among the most common types — nearly half (47%) of EMEA respondents identified education credentials as one of the areas where discrepancies are most frequently found [49].

Blockchain-based credential systems address the authenticity problem by anchoring credential hashes on a public ledger. Once a hash is recorded on-chain, any party can verify that the document is unchanged. Systems such as Blockcerts [1] and MIT's Digital Diplomas [2] have demonstrated the viability of this approach.

However, these systems introduce a new problem: over-disclosure. Sharing a hash-linked certificate reveals the institution, the degree, the date, and often the student's identity to any querying party. The data subject has no meaningful control over who learns what. This is incompatible with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [32], Article 5(1)(c), which mandates data minimisation — processing only data that is necessary for the stated purpose.

It is worth noting that storing credential hashes on-chain does not automatically remove all data-protection obligations. Where a hash is linkable to an identifiable student address, issuer, timestamp, or off-chain document, it may still constitute personal data. The system supports the data-minimisation principle by design but does not by itself guarantee GDPR compliance; this is not a legal compliance certification, and the remaining considerations are discussed in Section 7.6.

Core tension: Cryptographic authenticity and selective disclosure are both necessary but technically in conflict. A signature over a full credential proves authenticity at the cost of revealing everything; a zero-knowledge proof can prove a property of the credential without revealing the credential itself.

Zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) can resolve this tension. In a full ZK credential system, a prover holding a valid credential would construct a ZKP that convinces a verifier of a specific property — "this person holds a degree from a recognised institution issued after 2020" — without disclosing the degree type, the institution name, or the student's identity. Building such a system requires the ZK circuit to include a credential witness (hash, issuer signature, and validity status), which significantly increases circuit complexity.

This thesis designs and implements zkCredentials, which tackles the problem in two steps. Credential content privacy is achieved by storing only SHA-256 hashes on-chain — the documents themselves never touch the chain. Authentication privacy is achieved through a ZK Authentication Protocol: a user proves knowledge of a secret key corresponding to a registered commitment, without revealing the key. Per-session nullifiers are unlinkable through their values alone, although the authenticating wallet address (msg.sender) remains visible on-chain. This separates the credential registry from the ZK authentication layer and allows the system to be built and deployed on zkSync Era — a ZK rollup that provides EVM compatibility with dramatically lower transaction costs than Ethereum mainnet — within the scope of a diploma thesis.

1.2 Research Methodology

The research follows a design-science methodology [3], structured around four phases that build on each other:

  • Literature review: Survey of ZKP systems, on-chain credential schemes, and privacy-preserving authentication protocols published between 2016 and 2025.
  • Requirements elicitation: Identification of functional and non-functional requirements through analysis of shortcomings in existing systems and relevant regulatory constraints (GDPR [32], eIDAS 2.0 [33]).
  • Artefact construction: Design and implementation of the zkCredentials system — smart contracts, ZK circuit, and React frontend — using iterative development with a local Hardhat environment and deployment to zkSync Sepolia.
  • Evaluation: Correctness argument via property verification, security analysis relative to the stated threat model, and empirical measurement of proof generation latency and on-chain gas costs.

The implementation is open source and available at the project repository. All circuit constraint counts and gas figures cited in Chapter 7 were measured on reproducible deployments using the versions recorded in contracts/package.json.

1.3 Research Objectives

Four research questions drive the work, each with a measurable criterion evaluated in Chapter 7:

  • RO-1: Privacy-preserving credential access. How can a user authenticate to a credential-backed system without revealing their authentication secret or enabling an observer to link sessions? The ZK Authentication Protocol must hide the secret key and produce unlinkable session nullifiers. Credential content privacy is addressed separately through hash-only on-chain storage, ensuring raw credential data is never posted to the chain (evaluated in Section 7.1, SG-1 through SG-3).
  • RO-2: Replay-resistant authentication. How can ZK-based authentication be designed so that a captured proof cannot be reused? Each proof must be single-use, preventing session hijacking through proof replay (evaluated in Section 7.1, SG-4 and SG-5).
  • RO-3: Upgradeability without storage corruption. How can contract logic be updated without migrating stored credential data or disrupting active sessions? All contracts must use the UUPS proxy pattern with append-only storage layout (evaluated in Section 6.2).
  • RO-4: Practical gas efficiency on Layer-2. Is Groth16-based ZK verification economically viable on zkSync Era? Per-operation costs must meet the targets in Table 4.2 (NFR-02, NFR-03) and be below equivalent Ethereum mainnet costs (evaluated in Section 7.4).

1.5 Threat Model and Scope

Before describing what was built, it is worth being precise about what the system is designed to protect against — and what is explicitly outside its scope.

Trusted parties

The system administrator who deploys contracts and initialises the trusted setup is assumed to be honest. This is a significant trust assumption: the admin holds both DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE (OpenZeppelin's role manager) and ADMIN_ROLE, the latter of which controls contract upgrades and the verifier address in ZKAuthRegistry. A compromised admin key can silently substitute a malicious verifier that accepts any proof, or upgrade contract logic without user consent. As noted in Section 2.7, a production deployment must gate admin authority through a multi-signature wallet and a time-lock. The prototype does not do this; it is not trustless with respect to the administrator. Registered institutions (universities) are trusted to issue credentials only to their legitimate students. The Ethereum and zkSync L1/L2 networks are assumed to achieve consensus correctly.

Adversarial capabilities

An adversary may be a passive observer reading all on-chain data, an active attacker submitting arbitrary transactions, or a colluding verifier attempting to extract user identity from proofs. The adversary does not control the trusted setup (i.e., at least one participant in the Powers of Tau ceremony was honest).

Out of scope

Side-channel attacks on the client device, front-running of credential issuance transactions (i.e., an attacker observing and pre-empting an issueCertificate call — distinct from commitment registration front-running, which is addressed in Section 4.6), and social-engineering attacks against institution staff are outside the scope of this thesis. Key custody — how a user stores and backs up their ZK secret key — is a significant practical concern; it is acknowledged explicitly as a limitation in Section 7.6, along with concrete mitigations, but a production-grade solution is left as future work.

Chapter 2

Theoretical Background

The ZK authentication protocol in Chapter 5 rests on a specific combination of primitives: Groth16 proofs, the Poseidon hash function, and zkSync Era's BN254 precompile support. This chapter covers each in enough depth to make the design choices intelligible. Section 2.6 covers zkSync Era specifically; readers already comfortable with ZKPs can start there.

2.1 Zero-Knowledge Proofs

A zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) is an interactive or non-interactive protocol between a prover P and a verifier V. P wishes to convince V that a statement x is in a language L (i.e., that a witness w exists such that (x, w) ∈ R for some relation R), without revealing w. The protocol must satisfy three properties [4], [39]:

  • Completeness: If the statement is true and P knows w, then an honest V will always accept the proof.
  • Soundness: If the statement is false, no cheating P can convince an honest V to accept, except with negligible probability.
  • Zero-knowledge: V learns nothing about w beyond the fact that the statement is true. Formally, there exists a simulator S that can produce a transcript indistinguishable from a real proof without knowing w.

Zero-knowledge proofs were introduced by Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff (first presented at STOC 1985; journal version 1989 [4]). Their graph isomorphism protocol showed that an interactive protocol could convince a verifier of a statement's truth without revealing anything about why it is true — a result that was far from obvious at the time. Subsequent work extended this to NP-complete problems [5], establishing that any statement with an NP witness can in principle be proved in zero knowledge.

Non-interactive ZKPs

Interactive proofs require multiple rounds of communication between P and V. In many applications — including blockchain — interaction is infeasible. The Fiat–Shamir heuristic [6] transforms interactive proofs into non-interactive ones by replacing the verifier's random challenge with the output of a hash function applied to the prover's message. This transformation is secure in the random oracle model.

2.2 zk-SNARKs and Groth16

A zk-SNARK (Zero-Knowledge Succinct Non-Interactive Argument of Knowledge) is a non-interactive ZKP with two additional properties: succinctness (proof size is sublinear in the size of the statement) and knowledge soundness (the prover implicitly knows the witness, not just that one exists).

The Groth16 construction [7] is the most widely deployed zk-SNARK. It achieves the smallest known proof size for a general-purpose SNARK: a proof consists of exactly three group elements (two in G₁ and one in G₂ of the BN254 curve), totalling 256 bytes in uncompressed affine encoding (two 64-byte G₁ points plus one 128-byte G₂ point). Verification requires a single multi-pairing check (three pairing equations evaluated in one EIP-197 call) and is therefore constant time regardless of circuit size.

Trusted setup

Groth16 requires a circuit-specific trusted setup: a structured reference string (SRS) generated by a trusted party or multi-party ceremony. The SRS encodes encrypted powers of a secret τ. If τ is compromised, an adversary can produce false proofs. The "toxic waste" problem has led to large-scale ceremonies such as the Hermez Network Powers of Tau [27] and the Semaphore ceremony, which rely on the security assumption that at least one participant destroys their contribution.

BN254 elliptic curve

BN254 (also known as alt_bn128) is a pairing-friendly elliptic curve with 254-bit field size. It is the curve for which the Ethereum yellow paper [9] defines the ecAdd, ecMul, and ecPairing precompiled contracts (EIP-196 [10], EIP-197 [11]). This makes on-chain Groth16 proof verification feasible at approximately 180,000–260,000 gas for the pairing check alone; the total cost of an authenticate-and-update-state transaction is higher due to storage writes (see Section 7.4, Table 7.4).

2.3 Arithmetic Circuits and R1CS

A zk-SNARK operates over an arithmetic circuit defined over a finite field F_p. The circuit represents the computation to be proved as a directed acyclic graph of addition and multiplication gates. Each gate introduces one constraint.

The Rank-1 Constraint System (R1CS) [12] is the standard intermediate representation. An R1CS instance over F_p consists of matrices A, B, C ∈ F_p^{m×n} and a witness vector w ∈ F_p^n such that:

(A · w) ∘ (B · w) = C · w

where ∘ denotes the Hadamard (element-wise) product. Each row of A, B, C encodes one multiplication constraint. Addition constraints are "free" (they do not increase the number of rows). Circuit complexity is therefore measured in multiplication gates (R1CS constraints).

Circom

Circom [13] is a domain-specific language for writing arithmetic circuits that compile to R1CS. A Circom circuit consists of templates (reusable sub-circuits) and signals (field elements). The compiler outputs a .r1cs file and a witness calculator in WebAssembly, enabling browser-side proof generation.

2.4 Poseidon Hash Function

Poseidon [14] is a hash function designed specifically for ZK circuits. Classical hash functions such as SHA-256 or Keccak are efficient as software implementations but require tens of thousands of R1CS constraints per invocation (Keccak-256 requires approximately 150,000 constraints). This makes them prohibitively expensive inside ZK circuits.

Poseidon operates over a prime field F_p using a sponge construction with a permutation network consisting of round constants, a non-linear S-box layer (x^5 mod p), and a maximum distance separable (MDS) linear layer. The result is a hash function requiring approximately 250–600 R1CS constraints depending on the number of inputs (arity), a reduction of two to three orders of magnitude compared with SHA-256. In this thesis, empirical constraint counts range from ~474 for Poseidon(1) to ~589 for Poseidon(3) (see Section 5.4, Figure 5.1, and Appendix B for the full breakdown).

In this system, Poseidon is used for three purposes: hashing the secret key to derive its public commitment component, computing the commitment Poseidon(sk_hash, walletAddress, salt), and computing the nullifier Poseidon(sk, nullifierNonce). All three usages are compatible with the BN254 scalar field, ensuring constraint compatibility with the Groth16 circuit.

2.5 Blockchain and Smart Contracts

A blockchain is a distributed ledger maintained by a peer-to-peer network of nodes that agree on its state through a consensus protocol [40]. Ethereum [15] extended the blockchain model to support Turing-complete computation through the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM). Accounts on Ethereum are either externally owned accounts (EOAs) controlled by a private key or contract accounts whose code executes deterministically when called.

Smart contracts are programs deployed to contract accounts. They are immutable by default — once deployed, their bytecode cannot be changed. Known attack patterns against smart contracts (reentrancy, integer overflow, access control flaws) are extensively catalogued in the literature [41]. The UUPS proxy pattern (discussed in Section 2.7) is the standard solution to upgradeability within this constraint.

Access control

OpenZeppelin's AccessControl library [16], [42] provides role-based access control through a mapping from bytes32 role identifiers to sets of addresses. The onlyRole(ROLE) modifier restricts a function to callers who hold the specified role. This is used throughout the zkCredentials contracts to separate administrator, institution, employer, and student permissions.

2.6 Layer-2 Scaling and zkSync Era

Ethereum's throughput is limited to approximately 15 transactions per second at the base layer, with transaction fees rising sharply under load. Layer-2 scaling solutions execute transactions off-chain and post compressed state updates or validity proofs to Ethereum, inheriting its security while achieving higher throughput and lower fees.

ZK rollups [17] submit a zero-knowledge validity proof for each batch of transactions to a smart contract on Layer-1. If the proof is valid, the L1 contract accepts the state update. Unlike optimistic rollups, ZK rollups do not require a challenge period; finality is cryptographic rather than economic.

zkSync Era (chain ID 324, Sepolia testnet 300) [18] is a ZK rollup developed by Matter Labs. It is EVM-compatible: Solidity contracts compile with minor modifications, and the JSON-RPC API is largely identical to Ethereum's. Notable differences relevant to this project include:

  • Custom compiler (zksolc): Contracts must be compiled with zksolc rather than solc. The Hardhat plugin hardhat-zksync-deploy handles this automatically.
  • EVMLA flag restriction: The forceEVMLA compilation flag forces the EVM Legacy Assembly translation path in zksolc. For this verifier contract, the EVMLA path caused a "stack too deep" error due to the dense pairing assembly. Removing the flag enables zksolc's default Yul IR path, which handles deep stacks correctly (Section 6.6).
  • Pairing precompiles: The BN254 pairing precompiled contracts (EIP-197) are supported on zkSync Era, enabling on-chain Groth16 verification.

2.7 Upgradeable Smart Contracts (UUPS)

The UUPS (Universal Upgradeable Proxy Standard, EIP-1822) [19] pattern separates a contract's storage from its logic. A thin proxy contract stores state and delegates all calls to a logic (implementation) contract. Upgrading replaces the logic contract while the proxy — and therefore all stored data — remains at the same address.

In UUPS, the upgrade function lives in the implementation contract (unlike the Transparent Proxy pattern where it lives in the proxy). This reduces the proxy's bytecode and gas overhead. The _authorizeUpgrade function must be overridden to restrict who can perform upgrades; in this system, only the ADMIN_ROLE holder may call it.

Storage layout

Because the proxy delegates to different implementation addresses over time, storage layout must be consistent across versions. Variables must never be reordered or removed; new variables must be appended after existing ones. OpenZeppelin's @openzeppelin/contracts-upgradeable package [16] provides upgrade-safe base contracts and the __gap pattern to reserve storage slots for future use.

Upgradeability risks

The UUPS pattern is powerful but introduces trust assumptions that must be acknowledged. A compromised administrator key can silently replace contract logic with a malicious implementation. In ZKAuthRegistry specifically, the setVerifier() function allows the admin to substitute a different verifier contract — an attacker who controls the admin key could replace it with a verifier that accepts any proof. Additionally, implementation contracts must disable their initializers (_disableInitializers() in the constructor) to prevent re-initialisation attacks. In production, upgrade authority should be gated by a multi-signature wallet and a time-locked governance contract, giving users visibility and reaction time before a logic change takes effect. This thesis prototype uses a single admin key, which is acceptable for a research deployment but not for production. The prototype should therefore not be considered trustless with respect to the administrator.

3.2 ZK-Based Identity Systems

Semaphore

Semaphore [20] is an Ethereum-based protocol for anonymous signalling. Users register a commitment Poseidon(identityTrapdoor, identityNullifier) in a Merkle tree. To signal, they prove Merkle inclusion and reveal a nullifier hash. Semaphore's design directly inspired the commitment and nullifier pattern used in this thesis.

The key architectural difference is commitment storage. Semaphore places all commitments in an on-chain Merkle tree; proving membership in that tree hides which commitment belongs to which user, providing full anonymity within the group. This thesis stores commitments in a simple mapping(bytes32 => bool). The trade-off is deliberate: a mapping is cheaper to deploy and query (no on-chain tree updates, no Merkle path arguments in the circuit), and it ties each commitment explicitly to a wallet address — which is acceptable here because wallet-level anonymity was not a design goal (Section 1.5). If anonymity within a group becomes a requirement, the mapping could be replaced with a Semaphore-style Merkle tree in a future upgrade without changing the ZK circuit's commitment and nullifier structure. Semaphore is the natural extension point for future work on this system.

Polygon ID / Iden3

Iden3 [21] is a decentralised identity framework supporting W3C Verifiable Credentials [34] with ZK selective disclosure using Groth16. The Polygon ID implementation deploys on Polygon PoS. The system is production-grade and feature-rich but targets general identity; its credential schema language (JSON-LD [36] claims) and issuer infrastructure are significantly more complex than required for a single-institution academic credential use case. Polygon ID is the most directly comparable production system: it provides ZK credential proofs (not just ZK authentication) but does not deploy on zkSync Era, leaving a gap that this work addresses.

Zcash (Zerocash)

Zerocash [24] introduced the nullifier concept for privacy-preserving on-chain transactions, which directly inspires this work. In Zerocash, a nullifier is derived from a note's private key and prevents the same note from being spent twice — the analogy here is that the "note" is the user's identity credential and "spending" is logging in. Each fresh nullifierNonce produces a fresh, unlinkable nullifier. The Zcash cryptocurrency subsequently adopted Groth16 in its Sapling upgrade (2018), demonstrating the proof system's viability for production privacy protocols. The main structural difference from this work is that Zcash's circuit is also responsible for value conservation (proving input notes equal output notes in value), which is not relevant for authentication. Zcash also uses a Merkle tree of note commitments for anonymity, which this work trades for simpler commitment storage.

3.3 On-Chain Credential Schemes

Blockcerts

Blockcerts [1], developed by MIT and Learning Machine, anchors credential hashes in OP_RETURN outputs on Bitcoin or as Ethereum transactions. Verification is public: anyone with the certificate JSON and the on-chain hash can verify authenticity. Privacy is absent — the full certificate must be shared for verification.

Blockchain-Based Education Systems

Chen et al. [23] survey blockchain-based educational systems that use consortium blockchains for academic certificates among multiple universities. Credentials in such systems are typically stored as Solidity structs in non-upgradeable registry contracts, with no privacy or selective disclosure mechanisms.

Soulbound Tokens (EIP-5192)

Soulbound Tokens [43] are non-transferable on-chain tokens proposed as digital credentials. They provide on-chain provenance and non-transferability but permanently link credentials to a public wallet address, offering no privacy protections for the holder. The approach is conceptually simple but fundamentally incompatible with privacy: any observer who knows a wallet address can enumerate all credentials held by that identity.

3.4 Privacy-Preserving Authentication

zk-Auth patterns

The commitment-nullifier pattern used in this thesis draws on two prior lines of work. The nullifier concept originates in Zerocash [24], where a one-time tag is revealed on coin spending to prevent double-spending without linking spends to the same owner. Semaphore [20] adapts this idea to anonymous group signalling: a user registers a commitment in a Merkle tree and produces a per-signal nullifier to prevent double-signalling. The key insight common to both — that a per-session nonce produces independent nullifiers that prevent linkage even if an adversary records every on-chain value — is directly applied here for ZK authentication.

ZK identity systems with trusted credential issuers

Systems such as Proof of Passport [47] and Anon Aadhaar [48] use government-issued documents as the trusted root of identity, generating ZK proofs over digitally signed credential data. These systems achieve stronger identity binding (a ZK proof is anchored to a government-verified identity) but weaker privacy sovereignty (the government is involved in the trust chain). This thesis uses a self-generated private key as the root of trust. This is weaker in identity binding (anyone can generate a key, providing no Sybil resistance) but stronger in privacy sovereignty (no government involvement). The distinction is between ZK systems with a trusted credential issuer and those with self-sovereign identity.

AZTEC Protocol / Noir — a migration finding

This project originally used the Noir language (Aztec's ZK DSL) with the UltraPlonk proof system and the Barretenberg backend. The UltraPlonk verifier generated by Noir/Barretenberg was fundamentally incompatible with zkSync Era's zkEVM: the generated verifier contract required approximately 140 KB of bytecode and used assembly patterns that zksolc could not compile. This incompatibility led to the migration to Circom/Groth16. The finding itself — that proof system choice is coupled to the execution environment, not just to the cryptographic properties — is a practical contribution relevant to any team evaluating ZK deployment targets.

Proof system comparison

PLONK [25] uses a universal trusted setup — a single ceremony serves all circuits up to a maximum size, eliminating the per-circuit setup requirement of Groth16. PLONK's soundness rests on the algebraic group model (AGM) combined with the random oracle model, a slightly cleaner theoretical footing than Groth16's non-falsifiable q-PKE assumption. The trade-off is larger proofs (~768 bytes vs. Groth16's 256 bytes) and higher on-chain verification cost. The Groth16 choice in this thesis is justified by Ethereum's native ecPairing precompile support (EIP-196/197), which makes on-chain Groth16 verification the lowest-gas option available.

STARKs [8], [26] require no trusted setup and achieve post-quantum security (their security relies only on hash functions, aligning with the direction of NIST post-quantum standards [37]). The trade-off is proof size: a STARK proof is typically 40–200 KB compared with Groth16's 256 bytes. Verifying a STARK on-chain would cost significantly more gas than the ~170–200k gas for a Groth16 pairing check. For a system where every login is an on-chain transaction, verification cost dominates, making Groth16 the appropriate choice.

Bulletproofs [29] require no trusted setup and produce logarithmic-size proofs, but verification time is linear in the circuit size rather than constant. This makes Bulletproofs unsuitable for on-chain verification of circuits with more than a few hundred constraints. Ligero [31] is another transparent (no trusted setup) argument system, using interactive oracle proofs with sublinear communication; its verification cost is also too high for routine on-chain use. Cryptographic accumulators [30] offer an alternative approach to membership proofs with constant-size witnesses, relevant to future Merkle-based extensions (Section 8.2).

3.5 Gap Analysis and Positioning

Three gaps emerge from the survey, each corresponding to a contribution in Section 1.4:

  • Gap 1: No ZK credential system targets zkSync Era. Existing ZK identity systems deploy to Ethereum mainnet or Polygon. zkSync Era's lower fees make ZK verification economically accessible for low-value use cases such as academic credentials. Addressed by Contribution 3 (working deployment on zkSync Era Sepolia).
  • Gap 2: Upgradeability is absent in existing on-chain systems. Blockcerts and typical blockchain-education systems [23] deploy non-upgradeable contracts. Real deployments require the ability to fix bugs and extend functionality without migrating all stored credentials. Addressed by Contribution 1 (UUPS-upgradeable contract architecture).
  • Gap 3: Integration of ZK auth with credential management is unexplored. Semaphore and Polygon ID handle either anonymous signalling or credential presentation, not both. zkCredentials integrates credential issuance, storage, and ZK-authenticated access in a single system. Addressed by Contribution 2 (Groth16-based ZK authentication protocol).

4.2 Architecture Overview

4.3 Smart Contract Design

UpgradeableBase

CertificateRegistry and InstitutionRegistry inherit from UpgradeableBase, which composes OpenZeppelin's Initializable, AccessControlUpgradeable, and UUPSUpgradeable, plus version-tracking with an upgrade history array. Both child contracts add ReentrancyGuardUpgradeable directly. ZKAuthRegistry and EmployerRegistry achieve equivalent upgradeability by inheriting these OpenZeppelin contracts directly, without the version-tracking and upgrade-history features provided by UpgradeableBase. EmployerRegistry additionally includes ReentrancyGuardUpgradeable to protect its state-changing registration functions; ZKAuthRegistry omits ReentrancyGuardUpgradeable; its external calls (authVerifier.verify()) target a trusted, non-state-modifying verifier contract that cannot trigger re-entry, though adding reentrancy protection would follow defence-in-depth principles. In all four contracts, _authorizeUpgrade is restricted to ADMIN_ROLE.

InstitutionRegistry

Maintains a mapping from institution addresses to Institution structs containing name, emailDomain, walletAddress, isVerified, isActive, verificationDate, and totalCertificatesIssued. Institutions self-register via registerInstitution(name, emailDomain); the admin then approves, suspends, or reactivates them. Emits InstitutionRegistered, InstitutionApproved, InstitutionSuspended, and InstitutionReactivated events.

CertificateRegistry

Stores credential hashes as a mapping from uint256 certificateId to a Certificate struct. Only addresses that pass the institutionRegistry.canIssueCertificates(msg.sender) check — i.e., registered and active institutions — may issue certificates. The raw credential content is never stored on-chain; only the SHA-256 hash of the PDF document is stored. Revocation is supported: revokeCertificate() sets an isRevoked flag permanently — once revoked, a certificate cannot be reinstated. Emits CertificateIssued and CertificateRevoked.

EmployerRegistry

Manages employer registration with company details and VAT number uniqueness enforcement. Employers self-register via registerEmployer(companyName, vatNumber); the contract performs cross-contract role conflict validation (checking that the caller is not an admin, university, or student) before accepting the registration. Admin-only functions allow deactivation and reactivation of employer accounts.

ZKAuthRegistry

The central contract for the ZK authentication flow. Stores the deployed address of the Groth16 VerifierAdapter contract. Maintains:

  • mapping(bytes32 => bool) public commitments — whether a commitment is registered
  • mapping(bytes32 => bool) public usedNullifiers — replay prevention
  • mapping(bytes32 => Session) public sessions — session data keyed by sessionId

The startSession(bytes32 commitment, bytes calldata proof, bytes32 nullifierNonce, bytes32 nullifier) function accepts the caller-supplied commitment and the encoded Groth16 proof, verifies that commitments[commitment] is true (i.e., the commitment was previously registered), checks the nullifier against usedNullifiers, calls the verifier adapter with the public inputs [commitment, nullifierNonce, nullifier], and if valid, marks the nullifier as spent and creates a session with a 24-hour expiry. The session identifier is derived as sessionId = keccak256(commitment, block.timestamp, msg.sender, blockhash(block.number − 1), nullifier). Including the nullifier in the hash binds each session to the specific ZK proof that created it and adds proof-specific entropy independent of miner-controllable inputs such as blockhash. Wallet binding is not enforced at the contract level: the commitment is caller-supplied rather than retrieved from an address-to-commitment mapping. Security relies on the ZK proof itself — only a prover who possesses the private key, wallet address, and salt used to construct the commitment can generate a valid proof. Since the commitment was computed as Poseidon(Poseidon(privateKey), walletAddress, salt), these three secrets are required for authentication. The authenticating wallet address is visible on-chain in the transaction's msg.sender, making authentication pseudonymous at the wallet level, not anonymous.

4.4 Adversary Model

The system's security claims are stated with respect to a probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT) adversary who can:

  • Observe all on-chain state: commitments, nullifiers, session records, certificate hashes, and transaction calldata.
  • Submit arbitrary transactions: malformed proofs, replayed proof bytes, or any valid ABI-encoded calldata.
  • Control the network: reorder, delay, or front-run pending transactions in the mempool.

The system is not secure against an adversary who can:

  • Break the discrete logarithm problem on the BN254 elliptic curve.
  • Find a collision in the Poseidon hash function over the BN254 scalar field.
  • Compromise the trusted setup (retain the toxic waste from the Phase 2 ceremony).
  • Execute arbitrary JavaScript in the user's browser context (XSS, supply chain attack).

The formal security properties of the system — soundness, zero-knowledge, commitment binding and hiding — are argued by reduction to established computational hardness assumptions in Sections 7.1 and 7.2.

4.5 Security Goals

Under the adversary model above, the system is designed to achieve the following security properties. Formal arguments supporting each claim are provided in Section 7.1 (per-goal analysis) and Section 7.2 (composite security reduction).

  • SG-1 Commitment binding: A user cannot produce a valid proof for a commitment they did not generate, because the Poseidon hash is collision-resistant in the BN254 scalar field.
  • SG-2 Commitment hiding: An observer of the commitment value cannot determine the secret key, because the commitment includes a random salt and Poseidon is a one-way function. This is computational hiding, not information-theoretic (unlike Pedersen commitments [38]): it holds against PPT adversaries but not against unbounded ones.
  • SG-3 Proof soundness: No PPT adversary without knowledge of the correct sk can produce a proof accepted by the on-chain verifier, under the q-Power Knowledge of Exponent (q-PKE) assumption on BN254 [7]. The q-PKE assumption is non-falsifiable — it cannot be disproved even in principle — which is a known and accepted property of Groth16's security argument.
  • SG-4 Replay resistance: Each session uses a fresh nullifier nonce, producing a unique nullifier. The usedNullifiers mapping prevents reuse.
  • SG-5 Nullifier unlinkability: Two valid proofs from the same user produce different nullifiers because the nonce differs. An observer cannot link sessions from nullifier values alone. Wallet-level unlinkability is not achieved: msg.sender is visible in every authentication transaction.

The system explicitly does not claim:

  • Anonymity beyond commitment-level privacy (wallet addresses are visible on-chain; see Section 7.6).
  • Protection against a malicious or compromised frontend (see Section 4.6, Threat Model).
  • Sybil resistance (any address can register a commitment).
  • Forward secrecy (a compromised sk permits indefinite impersonation; see Section 7.6).

Chapter 5

ZK Authentication Protocol

The ZK Authentication Protocol is the novel element of this work. This chapter describes it end to end — from how a secret key is generated in the browser, through the Circom circuit that enforces the commitment and nullifier constraints, to how the resulting proof is ABI-encoded and verified on-chain.

5.1 Key Generation

During registration, the user's browser generates a 32-byte (256-bit) secret key sk using the Web Crypto API (crypto.getRandomValues). The value is reduced modulo the BN254 scalar field modulus p = 21888242871839275222246405745257275088548364400416034343698204186575808495617 to ensure it lies within F_p. Since p < 2254, the effective entropy after reduction is approximately 254 bits.

sk ← {0,1}^256 mod p   (giving sk < p, approximately 254-bit effective entropy)

The key is stored in the user's browser localStorage using AES-GCM encryption via the SubtleCrypto API. In the current prototype, the AES key is derived deterministically from the wallet address via SHA-256("zkcredentials:zkauth:v3:" + walletAddress). A wallet signature is requested before decryption as a UX gate (to confirm user intent), but the signature is not used as cryptographic key material in this version. This means the encryption key is derivable by anyone who knows the wallet address — which is public — reducing the protection to localStorage access control only. This is explicitly acknowledged as a limitation in Section 7.6. A production implementation should derive the AES key from a wallet signature via HKDF-SHA-256, as described in the research paper's recommended approach (Section IV-B of [45]), to ensure that only the wallet holder can decrypt the stored credentials.

5.2 Commitment Scheme

Rather than committing to sk directly, the system uses a two-level commitment to allow the circuit to verify key knowledge without revealing sk to the Solidity verifier.

sk_hash = Poseidon(sk) C = Poseidon(sk_hash, walletAddress, salt)

where salt is a fresh random field element generated at registration time. The commitment C is stored on-chain via ZKAuthRegistry.commitments[C] = true, recording that this commitment has been registered. It serves as the public key for the ZK authentication protocol.

Properties

Binding: The user cannot change sk after committing without producing a different C, since Poseidon is collision-resistant. Hiding: An adversary observing C cannot determine sk or sk_hash without inverting Poseidon, which requires solving a system of algebraic equations over F_p with approximately 254 bits of entropy in the input. The inclusion of walletAddress in the commitment binds the commitment to the user's on-chain identity, preventing commitment transfer.

5.3 Nullifier Mechanism

To prevent proof replay, each authentication session uses a unique nullifier derived from the secret key and a per-session nonce:

nullifierNonce ← {0,1}^256 mod p   (fresh per session, ~254-bit effective) N = Poseidon(sk, nullifierNonce)

The nullifier N is a public output of the ZK circuit. The ZKAuthRegistry.startSession function checks usedNullifiers[N] == false before accepting a proof, then sets usedNullifiers[N] = true. The nonce is also a public input to the circuit, allowing the verifier to confirm that the nullifier was derived with the correct nonce.

Because the nonce differs between sessions, two authentications by the same user produce different nullifiers. An observer cannot link sessions from nullifier values alone (SG-5). However, since both transactions are submitted by the same wallet, msg.sender links them at the wallet level. Nullifier unlinkability protects the secret key and prevents session correlation through proof content, not through wallet identity.

5.4 Circuit Design

The ZK Auth circuit is written in Circom 2. It has the following signal interface:

Table 5.1 — ZK Auth circuit signals

Constraint structure

The circuit enforces the following constraints:

  • Inner hash: sk_hash === Poseidon([privateKey]) — computes the intermediate hash of the secret key.
  • Commitment check: commitment === Poseidon([sk_hash, walletAddress, salt]) — verifies that the public commitment matches the private inputs.
  • Nullifier check: nullifier === Poseidon([privateKey, nullifierNonce]) — verifies that the nullifier is correctly derived.

Total R1CS constraints: 1,537 (as reported by snarkjs r1cs info). The circuit comprises three Poseidon invocations with varying state widths (t=2, t=4, and t=3 respectively) plus internal wiring. No explicit range check on sk is included in the current implementation; field overflow is mitigated by the 256-bit key generation procedure in the frontend, which reduces the value modulo the BN254 field modulus. Witness generation time in WebAssembly is approximately 40 ms; proof generation time in the browser is 2–8 seconds depending on device CPU.

Wallet binding: current design and limitations

The circuit does not verify that walletAddress (a private input) equals msg.sender — it cannot, because the circuit runs in the browser before the transaction is submitted, so msg.sender is not available as an input to the proof. The contract likewise does not enforce wallet binding: the commitment is caller-supplied, and the contract only verifies that the commitment is registered (commitments[commitment] == true) and that the ZK proof is valid. Consequently, any wallet in possession of the three secrets (privateKey, walletAddress, salt) can authenticate against a given commitment.

This is a deliberate design trade-off. Wallet binding is provided cryptographically rather than programmatically: since the commitment encodes a specific wallet address, only a prover who knows that address (along with the private key and salt) can construct a valid proof. In practice, the wallet address is known to the original registrant and is observable on-chain in the registration transaction, so the security argument reduces to knowledge of the private key and salt. A stronger enforcement — including msg.sender as a public input and constraining it to equal walletAddress within the circuit — would make each proof cryptographically non-transferable, tying authentication to a specific wallet independent of secret knowledge. This is identified as future work (Section 8.2).

Additionally, the circuit does not include a range check on walletAddress. Ethereum addresses are 20 bytes (160 bits), but the BN254 field accepts any element up to ~254 bits. A user could register a commitment with a walletAddress value exceeding 2160 − 1, which cannot correspond to any real Ethereum address. In practice this is self-defeating (the commitment would be permanently unusable), but it is a formal gap. A complete solution would add a 160-constraint range proof inside the circuit to enforce walletAddress < 2160.

5.5 Trusted Setup

Groth16 requires a two-phase trusted setup:

Phase 1 — Powers of Tau

The universal Powers of Tau ceremony produces a structured reference string encoding encrypted powers of a secret τ. This thesis uses a locally generated Powers of Tau ceremony (via snarkjs [27]) with 211 constraints (2,048 — sufficient for the circuit's 1,537 constraints). The ceremony output is the pot11_final.ptau file. A production deployment should use a community ceremony such as the Hermez Network's perpetual Powers of Tau [27] to distribute Phase 1 trust across multiple independent contributors.

Phase 2 — Circuit-specific setup

The circuit-specific setup uses snarkjs to derive the proving key (zkey) and verifying key (vkey) from the Powers of Tau file and the compiled R1CS:

snarkjs groth16 setup auth_login.r1cs pot11_final.ptau auth_login_0000.zkey snarkjs zkey contribute auth_login_0000.zkey auth_login_final.zkey --name="Contributor" snarkjs zkey export verificationkey auth_login_final.zkey verification_key.json

The resulting auth_login_final.zkey (proving key, ~2.4 MB) is served from the frontend's public assets directory. The verifying key is exported as a Solidity contract (Groth16AuthVerifier.sol) using snarkjs exportSolidityVerifier. For a production deployment, the Phase 2 contribution should involve multiple independent parties.

5.6 Proof Encoding and On-Chain Submission

snarkjs generates a Groth16 proof as a JSON object containing three curve points:

  • proof.pi_a: a G₁ point (2 field elements)
  • proof.pi_b: a G₂ point (2×2 field elements)
  • proof.pi_c: a G₁ point (2 field elements)

BN254's G₂ points are defined over a field extension Fp², meaning each coordinate is a pair (real, imaginary). snarkjs stores πB coordinates as [imaginary, real] in the JSON proof output, but the Solidity verifier (EIP-197 [11]) expects [real, imaginary]. The frontend transposes pi_b[0] and pi_b[1] before ABI encoding; failure to do so causes silent proof rejection. The proof and public inputs are ABI-encoded using viem's encodeAbiParameters:

proof = encodeAbiParameters([pA, pB, pC])

The ABI-encoded proof (256 bytes) is submitted together with the public inputs as separate arguments to ZKAuthRegistry.startSession(bytes32 commitment, bytes calldata proof, bytes32 nullifierNonce, bytes32 nullifier), which verifies the commitment is registered, checks nullifier uniqueness, calls Groth16AuthVerifierAdapter.verify(proof, publicInputs), and on success creates an authenticated session.

5.7 Session Lifecycle

The full session lifecycle comprises seven steps:

  • 1. Registration (once): User generates sk, salt; computes commitment C; generates a Groth16 proof and nullifier for the commitment; calls ZKAuthRegistry.registerCommitment(C, role, proof, nullifierNonce, nullifier); the contract verifies the proof before storing the commitment. The user stores (sk, salt) encrypted in browser storage.
  • 2. Auth initiation: User navigates to ZK login; frontend retrieves commitment from chain to confirm registration.
  • 3. Nonce generation: Frontend generates a fresh nullifierNonce using crypto.getRandomValues.
  • 4. Proof generation: Frontend loads proving key; calls snarkjs with private inputs (privateKey, walletAddress, salt) and public inputs (commitment, nullifierNonce, nullifier); generates proof.
  • 5. On-chain submission: Frontend ABI-encodes proof; submits transaction to ZKAuthRegistry.startSession; waits for confirmation.
  • 6. Session state: On success, the contract emits SessionStarted(bytes32 indexed sessionId, bytes32 indexed commitment, uint256 expiry); the frontend parses this event from the transaction receipt to extract the on-chain sessionId, stores it in the client auth store, and grants access to authenticated routes. The stored sessionId is used for subsequent endSession (logout) calls.
  • 7. Session revalidation on reload: On every application load, if a persisted ZK session exists in the client auth store, the frontend calls ZKAuthRegistry.validateSession(sessionId) to verify the session is still active and not expired on-chain. If the session is invalid, the persisted ZK authentication state is cleared before the route guard makes any access-control decisions. This prevents stale localStorage entries from granting authenticated access after session expiry or on-chain termination.

Chapter 7

Evaluation

With the system built and deployed, three questions need answering: Does it actually achieve its security goals? How fast is proof generation in practice? And what does on-chain verification cost? This chapter works through each in turn.

7.1 Correctness Argument

Rather than simply asserting that the system is secure, this section traces each security goal from Section 4.5 back to a standard computational hardness assumption. The arguments are informal reductions rather than formal proofs; producing the latter is identified as future work in Section 8.2.

SG-1: Commitment binding

Claim: No probabilistic polynomial-time adversary A can find two distinct inputs (sk, salt, addr) and (sk', salt', addr') such that Poseidon(Poseidon(sk), addr, salt) = Poseidon(Poseidon(sk'), addr', salt') with non-negligible probability.

Argument: The Poseidon permutation is a pseudorandom permutation over F_p. Finding a collision requires finding an input collision in the sponge construction, which is computationally equivalent to finding a fixed point of the internal permutation. The permutation's algebraic degree over F_p makes this infeasible under the assumption that solving high-degree polynomial systems over F_p is hard (related to the MiMC security analysis [28]).

SG-2: Commitment hiding

Claim: No adversary observing C = Poseidon(Poseidon(sk), addr, salt) can determine sk with better than negligible advantage.

Argument: sk is a 256-bit value reduced modulo the BN254 field, yielding approximately 254 bits of min-entropy. The salt is a fresh random field element independent of sk. The commitment is a deterministic function of (sk, addr, salt), but inverting Poseidon requires solving the degree-5 algebraic system imposed by the S-box rounds, which provides security equivalent to the algebraic immunity of the construction (128-bit security claimed by the Poseidon authors [14]). BN254 curve-level qualification: the 128-bit security claim refers to the Poseidon hash function's resistance to algebraic attacks. The BN254 elliptic curve itself provides approximately 100–110 bits of effective security against the discrete logarithm problem, not 128 bits, due to pairing-specific attacks such as the MOV attack [44] and the Cheon attack [46], which reduce the security of pairing groups below the generic group bound. This level remains sufficient for practical purposes and is the reason Ethereum adopted BN254 for its precompiles (EIP-196 [10], EIP-197 [11]). The alternative, BLS12-381 (used by Ethereum's beacon chain), provides closer to 128-bit security but lacks EVM precompile support, making on-chain verification prohibitively expensive.

SG-3: Proof soundness

Claim: No adversary without knowledge of sk can produce a proof accepted by the on-chain verifier for a commitment C with non-negligible probability.

Argument: This follows directly from the knowledge soundness of Groth16, as formalised by Groth [7, Theorem 1]. Under the q-Power Knowledge of Exponent (q-PKE) assumption on BN254 — stated as Assumption 2 in [7] — any prover that produces an accepted proof implicitly knows an extractable witness. The verifier checks three pairing equations via a single multi-pairing precompile call. Producing a forged proof that satisfies the verification equation is infeasible under the q-PKE assumption; informally, this relates to the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem in pairing groups on BN254, for which no sub-exponential algorithm is known.

SG-4: Replay resistance

Claim: A valid proof cannot be resubmitted to obtain a second authenticated session.

Argument: The nullifier N = Poseidon(sk, nullifierNonce) is a public output of the proof. The ZKAuthRegistry.startSession function checks usedNullifiers[N] == false and atomically sets it to true within the same transaction. Any resubmission of the same calldata will produce the same nullifier N (since the proof encodes the same public inputs), which will be rejected. This is an unconditional check with no adversary being able to bypass it without a hash collision (SG-1).

SG-5: Nullifier unlinkability

Claim: Two valid proofs from the same user cannot be linked by nullifier values alone.

Argument: Each session uses a fresh nullifierNonce ← {0,1}256 mod p (approximately 254-bit effective entropy). The nullifier N = Poseidon(sk, nullifierNonce) differs between sessions because the nonce differs. An adversary observing N₁ and N₂ on-chain cannot determine whether they come from the same sk without inverting Poseidon (SG-2). Scope limitation: because both authentication transactions are submitted from the same wallet, msg.sender links them at the wallet level regardless of nullifier values. SG-5 therefore provides nullifier-level unlinkability only, not wallet-level anonymity. Full wallet-level unlinkability would require a relayer or a Merkle-based anonymity set (Section 7.6).

7.2 Security Analysis

Composite security reduction

The security of ZKAuthRegistry's authentication guarantee reduces to three independent sub-problems:

  1. Groth16 soundness under the q-Power Knowledge of Exponent (q-PKE) assumption on BN254 [7]. If this assumption is broken, an adversary can forge proofs and impersonate any registered user without knowledge of their private key.
  2. Poseidon-128 collision resistance over the BN254 scalar field [14]. If this is broken, an adversary can forge commitments — finding a different (sk', addr', salt') tuple that produces the same commitment as the victim.
  3. AES-GCM-256 security for local credential storage. If this is broken, an adversary with access to the user's localStorage can extract the secret key directly. Caveat: this assumption requires a properly derived encryption key. In the current prototype, the AES key is derived from the public wallet address (Section 5.1), which does not satisfy the assumption — localStorage access alone suffices to decrypt. A production deployment must use HKDF-SHA-256 over a wallet signature to restore the full strength of this assumption (see Section 7.6).

The system makes no additional cryptographic assumptions beyond these three. If any one of the three primitives is broken, the corresponding guarantee fails independently; the remaining guarantees are unaffected.

Note on non-falsifiable assumptions: the q-PKE assumption underlying Groth16 is non-falsifiable — it cannot be disproved even in principle, unlike standard computational assumptions (e.g., discrete log, CDH) that admit reduction-based security proofs. This is a known and accepted property of Groth16 in the literature. The practical response is that KEA-type assumptions have been used in cryptographic constructions for decades without a known break, and the pairing-based assumptions on BN254 have been extensively analysed. An alternative system using PLONK would achieve soundness under the algebraic group model (AGM) combined with the random oracle model — a slightly cleaner theoretical footing — at the cost of larger proofs (~768 bytes vs. 256) and higher on-chain verification cost.

7.6 Privacy Analysis and Limitations

Formal privacy terminology

The system achieves pseudonymity, not anonymity. The distinction is formally significant:

  • Anonymity: An observer cannot determine which user performed an action, even within the set of known users. The system does not achieve this.
  • Pseudonymity: An observer can link actions to a persistent pseudonym (here, the commitment) but not to a real-world identity. The system does achieve this — the commitment is the user's pseudonym.

Two specific privacy properties are worth naming formally:

  • Nullifier unlinkability (achieved): Each nullifier is Poseidon(sk, freshNonce) and is computationally independent of any other nullifier under the one-wayness of Poseidon; an observer cannot correlate sessions through the nullifier data alone. However, because the commitment is a public input to startSession and msg.sender is visible, sessions are linkable at the wallet level (see registration unlinkability below).
  • Registration unlinkability (not achieved): The user's wallet address (msg.sender) is permanently linked to their commitment on-chain via the registration transaction. Anyone who observed that transaction knows which commitment belongs to that address forever. Full registration unlinkability would require a Merkle tree of commitments (as in Semaphore [20]), where a user proves membership in the set without revealing which commitment is theirs. This design deliberately trades that stronger privacy guarantee for simpler implementation and lower gas cost.

Forward secrecy

The system has no forward secrecy. If a user's privateKey is compromised at any point, an attacker can:

  • Compute the user's commitment (confirming which on-chain commitment belongs to the victim).
  • Generate valid proofs and start authenticated sessions indefinitely from any wallet (since the contract does not enforce wallet-level binding — the commitment is caller-supplied and any wallet with the correct secrets can authenticate).
  • Reconstruct the link between all past nullifiers: for each observed nullifierNonce (a public input in every authentication transaction), the attacker computes Poseidon(sk, nullifierNonce) and matches the result against on-chain nullifiers, identifying every session the victim ever created.

The system provides session secrecy (a stolen session ID is useless after 24 hours) but not key secrecy (a stolen private key is useful indefinitely). The correct mitigation is a key rotation mechanism: register a new commitment, revoke the old one. Commitment revocation is not currently implemented — once a commitment is registered, it stays registered permanently. This is a known limitation identified as future work in Section 8.2.

  • Single-contributor trusted setup: The Phase 2 ceremony has one contributor. Mitigation: run a multi-party ceremony before any production deployment.
  • Revocation is permanent and non-reversible: CertificateRegistry implements revokeCertificate() which sets an isRevoked flag permanently. Once set, the flag cannot be unset — revocation is one-way by design. This is appropriate for most academic credential use cases where issued certificates should not be reinstatable after revocation.
  • Commitment non-updatable: A user whose sk is lost cannot replace their commitment. Mitigation: add an admin-authorized commitment reset with appropriate access controls in a future contract version.
  • Front-running on commitment registration: A malicious node could observe a pending registerCommitment transaction and submit the same commitment with a higher gas price. Mitigation: include the sender address in the commitment (already done via walletAddress binding), which makes the commitment address-specific.
  • Cryptographic vs. programmatic wallet binding: Wallet binding relies on secret knowledge (privateKey + salt) rather than on-chain enforcement. The commitment is caller-supplied, and the contract verifies only that it is registered and that the ZK proof is valid — it does not check msg.sender against the commitment. Any wallet with the correct secrets can authenticate. The stronger approach — including msg.sender as a public input and constraining it in the circuit — would make proofs cryptographically non-transferable independent of secret knowledge. See Section 5.4 for the full analysis.
  • No range check on walletAddress in circuit: The circuit accepts any BN254 field element as walletAddress, including values exceeding 2160 that cannot correspond to real Ethereum addresses. This is a formal gap; adding a 160-constraint range proof would close it.
  • Role not proved in the ZK circuit: The UserRole passed to registerCommitment is plain calldata — it is not a public input constrained by the circuit. Consequently, a user can register as any role (Student or Employer) regardless of their actual status. The circuit proves only commitment ownership, not role eligibility. A stronger design would include role as a public input and constrain it inside the circuit, or cross-check role-specific registries (EmployerRegistry, CertificateRegistry) within the ZK registration flow. This is left as future work.
  • ZK registration path bypasses registry-level role validation: In the Web3 authentication path, employers must pass VAT and role-conflict checks in EmployerRegistry.registerEmployer. In the ZK path, ZKAuthRegistry.registerCommitment stores roles[commitment] = Employer based solely on the caller’s choice, without checking EmployerRegistry. The two auth paths have intentionally different trust models: the ZK path trades role-validation rigour for privacy, accepting that role assignment relies on social/administrative trust rather than cryptographic enforcement.
  • Registration does not consume nullifiers: registerCommitment does not mark the nullifier as used in usedNullifiers, unlike startSession which does. The same proof/nullifier pair used during registration could theoretically be replayed as the first startSession call. In practice this is benign: the commitment itself prevents double-registration, and the session-level nullifier check prevents reuse after the first session. Adding usedNullifiers[nullifier] = true in registration would close this gap at a small additional gas cost.
Chapter 8

Conclusion and Future Work

The closing chapter does two things: it checks each research objective against what was actually built and measured, then lists what a production deployment would still need.

8.1 Summary of Contributions

zkCredentials set out to address the over-disclosure problem in blockchain credential systems — anchoring credentials publicly proves authenticity but exposes identity. Here is what was achieved against each research objective:

  • RO-1 achieved: A student can authenticate to the system without revealing their secret key, using a Groth16 ZKP over a Poseidon commitment to that key. Credential content privacy is achieved separately: raw credential data is never stored on-chain; only a SHA-256 hash of each credential document is anchored in the registry. The ZK circuit authenticates the user; it does not itself verify credential possession — that remains a target for future circuit extensions (Section 8.2).
  • RO-2 achieved: The ZK authentication protocol uses per-session nullifiers to prevent proof replay. Nullifier values are unlinkable across sessions; wallet-level linkability via msg.sender remains (as stated in SG-5).
  • RO-3 achieved: All four contracts use the UUPS proxy pattern with append-only storage layout and a storage gap; they have been upgraded in testing without data loss.
  • RO-4 achieved: ZK proof verification on zkSync Sepolia costs approximately 210,000 gas — within the NFR-02 budget (<350,000 gas) — at a real cost of approximately $0.01–0.05 at representative L2 gas prices, demonstrating economic viability on Layer-2. This result was made possible by resolving the forceEVMLA compiler flag incompatibility that initially blocked Groth16 verifier deployment on zkSync Era (Section 6.6).

The most technically involved piece is the ZK Authentication Protocol in Chapter 5: integrating a commitment-nullifier scheme with Groth16 and making it work on zkSync Era required resolving a compiler flag incompatibility (forceEVMLA) that the existing literature does not document. That compatibility fix is one of the more practically useful findings of the thesis.

The system is deployed and verified on zkSync Sepolia. All contracts, circuit files, and the frontend are in the project repository.

8.2 Future Work

A working prototype leaves a number of gaps that would matter in production. The items below are ordered roughly by how urgent they would be before going live:

  • Multi-party Phase 2 ceremony (highest priority): Run a coordinated Powers of Tau Phase 2 contribution with at least five independent parties before any production deployment. This is the most urgent prerequisite: the current single-contributor setup means the system's soundness rests entirely on one party's honesty, which is unacceptable outside a research prototype.
  • Hardware key storage: Integrate WebAuthn FIDO2 for binding sk to a hardware authenticator, preventing key extraction from browser storage under XSS or device-compromise attacks. As noted in Section 7.6, key custody is the most critical real-world deployment risk.
  • Selective credential disclosure: Extend the circuit to support proving specific attributes of a credential (e.g., "degree level is MSc" or "institution is in the EU") without revealing the full credential. This requires a more complex circuit with attribute decomposition.
  • ZK-provable revocation status: The CertificateRegistry already supports on-chain revocation via an isRevoked flag. The next step is to extend the ZK circuit to include a Merkle non-membership proof, allowing a user to prove their credential is not revoked without querying the registry directly during proof generation.
  • Universal trusted setup migration: Migrate from Groth16 to PLONK or Halo2 to eliminate the per-circuit trusted setup requirement entirely, at the cost of slightly larger proofs and higher on-chain verification gas.
  • W3C Verifiable Credentials integration: Align the credential schema with the W3C VC Data Model 2.0 [34] standard to enable interoperability with existing DID [35] ecosystems such as Polygon ID.
  • Formal verification: Apply formal methods (e.g., Certora Prover or Echidna) to the smart contracts, and a ZK circuit verification tool (e.g., Picus or CIVER) to the Circom circuit.

Building this system made clear how much friction still exists between ZK theory and practice. The forceEVMLA incompatibility, the G₂ coordinate reversal, and the single-contributor trusted setup are each small problems that a production team would solve quickly, but they each cost real time. The core architecture — credential anchoring plus ZK-authenticated access on a low-cost L2 — holds up well. Getting it to production quality is primarily an engineering problem at this point, not a research one.
