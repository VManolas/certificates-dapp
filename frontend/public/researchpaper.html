Privacy-Preserving Credential Verification
using Zero-Knowledge Proofs on zkSync Era
Vasileios Manolas
Department of Informatics and Computer Engineering
University of West Attica — Diploma Thesis
Submitted: May 2026  •  Supervisor: Dimitrios Kogias, Adjunct Lecturer
Abstract— This paper presents zkCredentials, a decentralised educational credential management and verification system deployed on zkSync Era. The system uses Groth16 zero-knowledge proofs to provide ZK-authenticated credential access: a user proves knowledge of a secret key corresponding to a registered on-chain commitment without revealing the key. Fresh Poseidon-based nullifiers prevent replay and avoid linkage through nullifier values, while credential content privacy is handled separately through hash-only on-chain storage. The current circuit does not prove credential possession directly; credential validity is checked through registry logic. Four UUPS upgradeable smart contracts manage institution registration, certificate issuance, employer access, and ZK-based session management. Evaluation on zkSync Sepolia shows proof generation in 2.1–7.8 s across representative devices and on-chain ZK verification at approximately 210,000 gas. We also document a zksolc compatibility finding that blocked a prior UltraPlonk-based approach, explaining why Groth16 over BN254 was selected for this deployment target.
Index Terms—Zero-Knowledge Proofs, Groth16, Circom, zkSync Era, Verifiable Credentials, Blockchain, Privacy, Poseidon Hash, UUPS Upgradeable Contracts, BN254, Decentralised Identity, Layer-2 Scaling.

I. Introduction

A. Motivation

Academic and professional credentials are a cornerstone of modern knowledge economies. Yet the infrastructure for issuing, storing, and verifying these credentials remains largely paper-based or centralised: a university mails a PDF, an employer emails a verification request, and a third-party database returns a binary valid/invalid response. Each step leaks identity data, introduces a single point of failure, and trusts intermediaries to behave honestly.

Blockchain technology offers an immutable, permissionless ledger for credential records, eliminating intermediary trust. However, naively placing credentials on a public chain exposes student identities to any observer, conflicting with the GDPR data-minimisation principle (Article 5(1)(c)) [18]. Zero-knowledge proofs provide a cryptographic primitive that can resolve this tension: in a full ZK credential system, a prover would convince a verifier of a statement such as “I hold a valid certificate from this institution” without revealing the underlying certificate data. This paper implements a narrower but deployable first step: a ZK-authenticated access protocol in which the user proves knowledge of a secret key bound to an on-chain commitment, while credential validity is checked separately through registry lookups.

B. Research Objectives

This paper pursues four primary objectives:

  1. Design and implement a privacy-preserving authentication protocol for blockchain-native credential access using Groth16 ZKPs.
  2. Build a suite of upgradeable smart contracts that enforce role-based access and credential lifecycle management on a production-grade Layer-2 network (zkSync Era).
  3. Evaluate the system’s security properties, gas costs, and proof generation performance against comparable approaches.
  4. Identify and explicitly bound the privacy guarantees provided, including known limitations of the commitment-based approach.

C. Contributions

The principal contributions of this work are:

  • A Circom circuit implementing a Poseidon-based commitment and nullifier scheme for replay-resistant ZK authentication (1,537 R1CS constraints).
  • A Groth16 on-chain verifier adapted for zkSync Era’s zkEVM, resolving incompatibility between the snarkjs-generated verifier’s inline assembly and the zksolc compiler’s EVMLA translation path.
  • Four UUPS upgradeable contracts deployed on zkSync Era Sepolia testnet, with role-based access control, cross-contract authorisation, and safe storage layouts.
  • A React/TypeScript frontend integrating snarkjs proof generation with wagmi wallet connection, supporting both ZK-native and standard Web3 authentication flows.
  • An empirical evaluation of proof generation time, on-chain verification gas cost, and security properties with explicitly bounded privacy claims and identified mitigations.

II. Background and Related Work

A. Zero-Knowledge Proof Systems

A zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) is an interactive or non-interactive protocol allowing a prover P to convince a verifier V that a statement x belongs to a language L, without leaking any information about the witness w beyond its existence [1]. Formally, a ZKP must satisfy: completeness (an honest prover always convinces an honest verifier), soundness (a cheating prover cannot convince the verifier of a false statement except with negligible probability), and zero-knowledge (the verifier learns nothing beyond the truth of the statement). Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson [23] established that any NP statement has a zero-knowledge proof system, and Fiat and Shamir [24] showed how to make such proofs non-interactive in the random oracle model.

zk-SNARKs (Zero-Knowledge Succinct Non-interactive ARguments of Knowledge) are particularly practical: they are non-interactive, produce constant-size proofs, and verify in constant time. Groth16 [2] is the most efficient known zk-SNARK construction, producing proofs consisting of three elliptic curve points (two in G1, one in G2), totalling 256 bytes in uncompressed affine encoding (two 64-byte G1 points plus one 128-byte G2 point), with verification requiring a single multi-pairing check (three pairing equations evaluated in one EIP-197 precompile call).

Groth16 security relies on the q-type Power Knowledge of Exponent (q-PKE) assumption in the generic group model [2]. BN254 provides approximately 100–110 bits of effective security due to the MOV reduction on pairing-friendly curves [3], which is acceptable for this application. Alternative systems include PLONK [21] (universal trusted setup, larger proofs), STARKs [22] (no trusted setup, post-quantum, 40–200 KB proofs), and Bulletproofs [25] (no trusted setup, logarithmic proof size, no succinct verification). Groth16 is chosen here because Ethereum’s [13] BN254 precompiles (EIP-196 [14], EIP-197 [15]) make on-chain Groth16 verification the lowest-gas option available.

B. The Circom/snarkjs Toolchain

Circom [4] is a domain-specific language for writing arithmetic circuits over finite fields. A Circom program compiles to a Rank-1 Constraint System (R1CS): (A · z) ∘ (B · z) = C · z where z is the combined witness/input vector and ∘ denotes the Hadamard product. snarkjs [5] provides the JavaScript/WASM implementation of Groth16 proof generation and verification, as well as tooling for the Powers of Tau ceremony and circuit-specific setup. Its browser-native WASM execution makes it suitable for privacy-sensitive applications where the proving key must never be sent to a server.

C. Poseidon Hash Function

The Poseidon hash function [6] is a sponge construction designed for minimal R1CS constraint count. Over BN254’s scalar field, Poseidon requires approximately 250–600 R1CS constraints depending on input arity (empirically ~474 for a single-input hash with t=2 state width in this circuit), compared to approximately 25,000 for SHA-256 and 150,000 for Keccak-256. This two-to-three order-of-magnitude reduction is critical for practical proving times on consumer hardware. The construction achieves approximately 128-bit security under the algebraic attack model for its parameter set over BN254.

D. zkSync Era and Layer-2 Scaling

zkSync Era [7] is a ZK-rollup Layer-2 network for Ethereum. It batches transactions off-chain, generates a ZK validity proof for each batch, and posts a compact state diff to Ethereum L1 for data availability and settlement. Users pay fees in ETH at gas costs roughly 10–50× lower than Ethereum mainnet. It is important to distinguish two separate layers of ZK proofs: (i) the rollup-level proof that zkSync Era itself generates to attest to batch correctness, which uses a STARK-based prover (Boojum), and (ii) the application-level Groth16 proofs generated by zkCredentials users and verified by a Solidity contract running on top of zkSync Era. These are independent proof systems; the rollup proof is invisible to the application.

The constraint relevant to this project: zksolc’s forceEVMLA flag, which forces EVM legacy assembly translation, causes stack-too-deep errors in the dense assembly of Groth16 verifiers. The fix is to use zksolc’s default Yul IR compilation path, which handles deep stacks correctly via the IR lowering pass (Section VI-B).

III. System Architecture

A. Overview

The system consists of three layers (Fig. 1): a client layer (React single-page application running in the browser), a contract layer (four UUPS upgradeable Solidity contracts on zkSync Era Sepolia testnet), and an optional indexing layer (reserved for a subgraph, not yet active).

The client layer handles all ZK proof generation locally: the user’s secret key never leaves the browser. Proofs are submitted to the contract layer for on-chain verification. Credential data (document hashes) is stored on-chain; actual documents are stored off-chain with only their SHA-256 hash committed to the blockchain.

B. Smart Contract Layer

Four contracts implement the core business logic:

  • InstitutionRegistry — manages institution registration, approval, and suspension.
  • CertificateRegistry — manages certificate issuance, revocation, and querying.
  • EmployerRegistry — manages employer registration with role-conflict prevention.
  • ZKAuthRegistry — manages ZK commitment registration, session creation, and nullifier tracking.

CertificateRegistry and InstitutionRegistry inherit from UpgradeableBase, which provides UUPS proxy support (EIP-1822 [16]) and OpenZeppelin’s AccessControlUpgradeable [17], plus version-tracking with an upgrade history array. Both child contracts add ReentrancyGuardUpgradeable directly. ZKAuthRegistry and EmployerRegistry achieve equivalent upgradeability through direct OpenZeppelin inheritance. Cross-contract authorisation is enforced via dedicated roles (e.g., CERTIFICATE_REGISTRY_ROLE on InstitutionRegistry permits only the CertificateRegistry contract to increment certificate counters).

C. ZK Proof System

The ZK proof system consists of:

  1. A Circom circuit (auth_login.circom) expressing the commitment and nullifier relations as 1,537 R1CS constraints.
  2. A Groth16 trusted setup producing a proving key (auth_login_final.zkey, ∼2.4 MB) and a verification key embedded in the on-chain verifier.
  3. A WASM witness generator served as a static asset for client-side proof generation.
  4. A Solidity verifier (Groth16AuthVerifier.sol) generated by snarkjs, calling the BN254 ecPairing precompile (EIP-197 [15]).
  5. An adapter contract (Groth16AuthVerifierAdapter.sol) translating the verifier’s ABI to the IAuthVerifier interface expected by ZKAuthRegistry.

D. Frontend Architecture

The frontend is a React 18 + TypeScript SPA built with Vite. It uses wagmi for Ethereum wallet connection, RainbowKit for the wallet UI, and React Query for blockchain state management. Heavy operations (ZK proof generation, PDF SHA-256 hashing) are kept off the main thread: proof generation via dynamic import('snarkjs'), PDF hashing via a Web Worker (pdfHashWorker.ts).

Role-based routing exposes distinct dashboards for admin, university, student, and employer personas, with guards preventing unauthorised access.

IV. ZK Authentication Protocol

A. Adversary Model and Privacy Goals

The ZK authentication protocol (Fig. 2) operates in two phases: a one-time commitment registration and a per-session authentication. Security claims are stated with respect to a probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT) adversary who can observe all on-chain state, submit arbitrary transactions, and control transaction ordering. The system is not secure against an adversary who can break the discrete logarithm problem on BN254 or find a Poseidon collision. Five classes of adversary are considered:

Passive blockchain observer: reads all on-chain state and transaction calldata, including the commitment registration transaction and all authentication transactions. Can observe msg.sender of every transaction. Partially mitigated: cannot determine private keys, salts, or link sessions from nullifier values alone. Can link sessions to the same wallet via msg.sender.

Active forger: submits arbitrary transactions to the contracts, including malformed proofs or replayed proof bytes, attempting to authenticate without knowledge of sk. Fully mitigated: Groth16 soundness (under q-PKE) prevents proof forgery; nullifier uniqueness prevents replay.

Malicious institution: issues fraudulent certificates for arbitrary student addresses and document hashes. Not mitigated by contract logic (the contract does not validate document authenticity); mitigated by admin suspension.

Compromised admin key: can upgrade registry implementations, approve fraudulent institutions, or call setVerifier() to replace the verifier adapter. Cannot forge ZK proofs (the Groth16Verifier is immutable). Most significant residual risk; mitigated in production by a multi-sig wallet and timelock controller.

Malicious frontend: can read sk from JavaScript memory during proof generation. Not mitigated; a browser extension or hardware enclave is out of scope.

Security reduction. The system’s authentication guarantee reduces to three independent sub-problems: (1) Groth16 soundness under the q-PKE assumption on BN254 [2], (2) Poseidon-128 collision resistance over the BN254 scalar field [6], and (3) AES-GCM-256 security for local credential storage (assuming proper key derivation; the prototype’s address-based key derivation does not satisfy this assumption — see Section IV-B and Section VII-D for the acknowledged limitation and the recommended signature-based HKDF fix). The system makes no additional cryptographic assumptions.

Privacy properties achieved: (i) The secret key sk and credential contents are never revealed on-chain. (ii) Two authentication sessions cannot be linked by their ZK proof contents or nullifier values alone (session unlinkability). (iii) An observer who does not know sk cannot determine what credential a user holds from their authentication transaction.

Privacy properties not achieved: Because authentication transactions are submitted by the user’s wallet, msg.sender is visible on-chain. An observer who observed the commitment registration transaction can therefore permanently link the authenticating wallet to the commitment (registration unlinkability is not achieved). Full wallet-level unlinkability would require a relayer or Merkle-based anonymity set (Section VIII-A). We do not protect against an adversary who learns the user’s secret key; such an adversary can forge proofs and impersonate the user indefinitely (no forward secrecy). The system achieves pseudonymity (actions linked to a persistent pseudonym — the commitment) rather than anonymity (actions unlinkable to any user identity).

B. Key Material Generation

The user generates a 256-bit secret key sk using the browser’s crypto.getRandomValues API, reduced modulo the BN254 scalar field modulus (p < 2254), yielding approximately 254 bits of effective entropy. A Poseidon-derived intermediate hash sk_hash = Poseidon(sk) is used in the commitment but never stored on-chain.

The secret key is encrypted client-side using AES-GCM with a 256-bit key. In the current prototype, the AES key is derived deterministically from the wallet address string via SHA-256; a wallet signature is requested as a UX gate but is not used as key material. This provides localStorage-level protection only. The recommended production approach is to derive the AES key from a deterministic wallet signature: the user signs a fixed domain string (e.g., "zkCredentials key derivation v1") with their wallet via personal_sign, and the 64-byte signature is passed through HKDF-SHA-256 to produce the AES-GCM key. This would tie key persistence to wallet ownership without uploading sk to any server, while ensuring the encryption key has sufficient entropy. The prototype’s weaker key derivation is explicitly acknowledged as a known limitation (Section VII-D).

Limitation: The current prototype derives the AES key from the wallet address rather than from a wallet signature, meaning any party who knows the address could derive the same key. A production deployment should use the signature-based HKDF derivation described above. Additionally, migrating from personal_sign to EIP-712 structured signing would display a human-readable description of what is being signed, improving user clarity and reducing phishing risk.

C. Commitment Scheme

Upon registration, the user computes: C = Poseidon(Poseidon(sk), walletAddress, salt) where walletAddress is the user’s Ethereum address (as a BN254 field element) and salt is a fresh 254-bit random value. The commitment C is submitted on-chain. Its properties are:

  • Computationally binding: under Poseidon collision resistance over BN254, it is infeasible to find a different (sk′, addr′, salt′) producing the same C.
  • Computationally hiding: given only C, an observer cannot determine sk or salt; Poseidon acts as a one-way function over BN254’s prime field. The salt provides 254-bit blinding entropy, ensuring that even low-entropy secrets are computationally hidden. Note that “computationally hiding” is the correct claim: Poseidon is not information-theoretically hiding against unbounded adversaries.

The binding to walletAddress is cryptographic rather than programmatic: the commitment is caller-supplied, and the contract verifies only that commitments[C] == true and that the ZK proof is valid — it does not check msg.sender against the address embedded in the commitment. Any wallet with the correct (sk, walletAddress, salt) can authenticate. The stronger approach — including msg.sender as a public input and constraining it in the circuit — would make proofs cryptographically non-transferable independent of secret knowledge, and is left as future work.

D. Nullifier Design

Each proof submission includes a fresh nullifier nonce n ( 256-bit random value reduced mod p, chosen by the prover) and a nullifier: N = Poseidon(sk, n) The circuit enforces this relation. The contract stores N after first use (usedNullifiers[N] = true), preventing replay of the same proof bytes. Because each session uses a fresh n, each session produces a fresh, computationally unlinkable N. Under the one-wayness of Poseidon, an on-chain observer cannot determine whether two nullifiers originate from the same sk without computing a preimage.

Registration does not enforce nullifier uniqueness; the commitment prevents double-registration. Session creation enforces nullifier uniqueness, preventing a single proof from opening multiple sessions.

E. Circuit Design

The Circom circuit (auth_login.circom) defines the following private inputs: privateKey, walletAddress, salt; and public inputs: commitment, nullifierNonce, nullifier. The commitment is caller-supplied; the contract verifies that it is registered (commitments[commitment] == true) and that the ZK proof is valid against the public inputs. Wallet binding is cryptographic rather than programmatic: only a prover who knows the private key, wallet address, and salt used to construct the commitment can generate a valid proof. The authenticating wallet address is visible on-chain via msg.sender; authentication is pseudonymous at the wallet level, not anonymous (see Section IV-A). The circuit enforces three relations:

  1. sk_hash = Poseidon(privateKey)
  2. commitment === Poseidon(sk_hash, walletAddress, salt)
  3. nullifier === Poseidon(privateKey, nullifierNonce)

F. Trusted Setup

Groth16 requires a circuit-specific trusted setup. The universal phase (Powers of Tau ceremony) uses a locally generated pot11_final.ptau file (supporting circuits up to 211 = 2,048 constraints, sufficient for the circuit’s 1,537), generated via snarkjs [12]. A production deployment should use a community ceremony such as the Hermez perpetual Powers of Tau. The circuit-specific phase was run locally using snarkjs, producing auth_login_final.zkey. In a production deployment this phase must be replaced with a multi-party computation (MPC) ceremony to distribute trust: the current single-contributor setup means security rests on the assumption that the contributor discarded the toxic waste.

G. Proof Encoding for On-Chain Verification

snarkjs outputs a Groth16 proof as three affine points: πA ∈ G1 (2 field elements), πB ∈ G2 (2×2 field elements), and πC ∈ G1 (2 field elements). A critical implementation detail: snarkjs stores πB coordinates as [imaginary, real], while the Solidity verifier (EIP-197 [15]) expects [real, imaginary]. Transposing these coordinates before ABI encoding is mandatory; failure causes silent proof rejection.

The proof is ABI-encoded using viem’s encodeAbiParameters: bytes = abi.encode(uint[2] pA, uint[2][2] pB, uint[2] pC) and passed as bytes calldata proof to the verifier.

H. Session Lifecycle

A session is created by calling ZKAuthRegistry.startSession(bytes32 commitment, bytes calldata proof, bytes32 nullifierNonce, bytes32 nullifier) with the registered commitment and a valid proof. On success, the contract records the nullifier as spent and generates a unique session identifier from keccak256(commitment, timestamp, msg.sender, blockhash, nullifier). Including the nullifier binds each session to the specific ZK proof that created it and adds proof-specific entropy independent of miner-controllable inputs. Sessions expire after 24 hours, checked at query time using block.timestamp. Logout calls endSession(bytes32 sessionId), which sets session.active = false immediately.

V. Smart Contract Design

A. Upgradeable Architecture

All four registry contracts use the UUPS pattern (EIP-1822 [16]) rather than Transparent Proxy. (The Groth16Verifier and its adapter are non-upgradeable plain contracts.) In UUPS, the upgrade logic resides in the implementation contract, reducing deployment gas and eliminating proxy-admin role ambiguity. The _authorizeUpgrade function is restricted to ADMIN_ROLE.

Storage safety across upgrades is enforced through the storage gap pattern: each contract reserves uint256[N] private __gap (N = 44–49, adjusted per contract to account for declared state variables) at the end of its layout. New storage variables in future upgrades consume gap slots rather than colliding with existing variables. Storage variables are append-only; reordering or removal would corrupt proxy storage.

B. InstitutionRegistry

InstitutionRegistry maintains the list of accredited institutions. An institution submits a pending application; an admin calls approveInstitution(wallet) to activate it. The emailDomain field is enforced unique (via emailDomainToAddress mapping) to prevent duplicate registration. The canIssueCertificates(wallet) view function returns true only when isVerified && isActive.

Certificate count tracking is incremented via incrementCertificateCount(), restricted to CERTIFICATE_REGISTRY_ROLE. This role is granted to the CertificateRegistry address post-deployment.

C. CertificateRegistry

CertificateRegistry is the core credential store. Each certificate is identified by a monotonically increasing uint256 certificateId (starting from 1; 0 means “not found”). The document is identified on-chain by its SHA-256 hash (bytes32 documentHash); the hash-to-ID mapping prevents duplicate issuance for the same document.

Revocation is permanent and append-only: once isRevoked = true, the certificate cannot be reinstated. Batch issuance (issueCertificatesBatch) supports up to 100 certificates per transaction. Note that this function calls InstitutionRegistry.incrementCertificateCount() once per certificate within the loop, producing N cross-contract calls for a batch of N certificates. A future optimisation is to pass the batch count as a parameter and increment in a single call, reducing gas by approximately (N − 1) × 2,300 gas (a cold cross-contract call). D. EmployerRegistry

EmployerRegistry allows non-student, non-institution, non-admin addresses to register as employers. Role-conflict validation checks both CertificateRegistry and InstitutionRegistry at registration time, reverting if the address already holds another role. VAT numbers serve as a company uniqueness identifier, enforced via vatToWallet mapping.

E. ZKAuthRegistry

ZKAuthRegistry is the on-chain component of the ZK authentication protocol. It stores:

  • commitments: mapping from bytes32 commitment hash to bool (whether registered).
  • sessions: mapping from bytes32 sessionId to Session struct (commitment, expiry timestamp, active flag).
  • usedNullifiers: mapping from bytes32 nullifier to spent flag.

The authVerifier address is upgradeable by admin via setVerifier(), allowing the ZK proof system to be upgraded without redeploying ZKAuthRegistry and losing existing commitment registrations.

F. Security Invariants

Key invariants enforced by contract logic: no address can hold more than one on-chain role simultaneously; certificate revocation is monotonic (cannot be un-revoked); nullifiers are consumed at most once; session expiry is checked at query time (not at creation time, avoiding the need for state transitions triggered by external actors); and _authorizeUpgrade is restricted to ADMIN_ROLE (keccak256("ADMIN_ROLE")), preventing unauthorised logic replacement. Custom Solidity errors (e.g., NullifierAlreadyUsed()) are used in ZKAuthRegistry, CertificateRegistry, and InstitutionRegistry to save approximately 30–50% gas on revert paths compared with string reverts. EmployerRegistry uses string-based require reverts.

VI. Implementation

A. Development Environment

Contracts are developed with Hardhat and the @matterlabs/hardhat-zksync plugin suite. The zksolc compiler is used for zkSync-specific compilation. Local development uses the Hardhat node (chain ID 1337, port 8545) for rapid iteration; testnet deployment targets zkSync Sepolia (chain ID 300). The frontend is built with Vite 5, React 18, TypeScript (strict mode), and Tailwind CSS. snarkjs is excluded from Vite’s pre-bundling (optimizeDeps.exclude) and loaded dynamically on demand to avoid a large initial bundle. The WASM witness generator and proving key are served from frontend/public/ as static assets, referenced via import.meta.env.BASE_URL for GitHub Pages compatibility.

B. zkSync Era Compilation Fix

The snarkjs-generated Groth16AuthVerifier.sol uses dense inline assembly to call the ecPairing precompile (EIP-197 [15]). In this project’s configuration, compiling the verifier with forceEVMLA: true produced a “stack too deep: variable is 1 slot(s) too deep” error, because the EVMLA path translates EVM assembly directly to LLVM IR and cannot handle the large number of stack variables in the pairing loop. Removing forceEVMLA and using zksolc’s default Yul IR compilation path resolved the issue; the IR lowering pass handles deep stacks correctly. This is an empirical finding for this verifier and toolchain version, not a universal zkSync Era limitation. The change is documented in hardhat.config.ts with an explanatory comment.

C. Proof Generation Pipeline

The client-side proof generation pipeline proceeds as follows:

  1. Request a wallet signature (UX gate confirming user intent); derive the AES-GCM decryption key. (In the prototype, the key is derived from the wallet address; in a production deployment, it should be derived from the signature via HKDF-SHA-256.)
  2. Decrypt sk from localStorage using the AES-GCM key.
  3. Compute sk_hash = Poseidon(sk) using circomlibjs (pre-bundled for fast load).
  4. Generate a fresh nullifierNonce via crypto.getRandomValues.
  5. Dynamically import snarkjs (Groth16 module only).
  6. Call snarkjs.groth16.fullProve(inputs, wasmUrl, zkeyUrl).
  7. Transpose πB coordinates (imaginary/real swap for EIP-197 compatibility).
  8. ABI-encode with viem’s encodeAbiParameters.
  9. Submit transaction to ZKAuthRegistry.startSession(commitment, proof, nullifierNonce, nullifier).

D. Deployment

Deployment follows a sequential dependency order enforced by Hardhat deploy scripts:

  1. Deploy Groth16Verifier (plain contract, no proxy).
  2. Deploy Groth16AuthVerifierAdapter.
  3. Deploy InstitutionRegistry proxy.
  4. Deploy CertificateRegistry proxy.
  5. Deploy EmployerRegistry proxy; call setRegistries(institutionRegistry, certificateRegistry).
  6. Deploy ZKAuthRegistry proxy (depends on adapter address).
  7. Call InstitutionRegistry.setCertificateRegistry(addr) to grant CERTIFICATE_REGISTRY_ROLE.

C. Security Analysis

Proof soundness. A forged proof requires breaking the q-PKE assumption over BN254, believed to hold under sub-exponential adversaries. Effective security is approximately 100–110 bits due to the MOV attack on pairing-friendly curves [3].

Zero-knowledge. The Groth16 simulator can produce transcripts indistinguishable from real proofs without the witness, given the simulation trapdoor (trusted setup toxic waste). Practically, the verifier learns nothing beyond the truth of the three circuit constraints.

Commitment hiding and binding. Under Poseidon collision resistance over BN254’s prime field, the commitment is computationally binding and computationally hiding (not information-theoretically hiding). The 254-bit salt provides sufficient blinding entropy for computational security at the 128-bit security level claimed by the Poseidon authors [6].

Nullifier unlinkability. Each session produces N = Poseidon(sk, n) with a fresh 256-bit nonce n (reduced mod p). Under the one-wayness of Poseidon, different nonces yield computationally independent outputs. An observer cannot link two nullifiers to the same user without knowledge of sk.

Replay resistance. The usedNullifiers mapping is checked and set atomically within the authentication transaction. Any resubmission of the same proof bytes produces the same nullifier and is rejected. This is an unconditional check requiring no adversarial assumptions beyond hash collision resistance.

D. Privacy Analysis and Known Gaps

The system achieves pseudonymity, not anonymity. Pseudonymity means an observer can link actions to a persistent pseudonym (the commitment) but not to a real-world identity. Anonymity would require that the observer cannot determine which user performed an action, even within the set of known users. Two formal privacy properties are distinguished:

Nullifier unlinkability (achieved). Each nullifier N = Poseidon(sk, freshNonce) is computationally independent under Poseidon one-wayness; an observer cannot correlate sessions through the nullifier data alone. However, because the commitment is a public input to startSession and msg.sender is visible, sessions are linkable at the wallet level (see registration unlinkability below).

Registration unlinkability (not achieved). The commitment value is stored on-chain by the user’s wallet via registerCommitment. Authentication transactions are submitted from the same wallet (msg.sender is visible). A passive observer can therefore permanently link authentication sessions to the registering wallet. Full registration unlinkability requires either a relayer (to decouple msg.sender from the user’s wallet) or a Merkle-based anonymity set (as in Semaphore [9]) to hide the commitment among a set. This design deliberately trades stronger privacy for simpler implementation and lower gas cost.

No forward secrecy. If sk is compromised, all past sessions become retroactively linkable — an attacker can compute Poseidon(sk, n) for each observed public nonce n and match the results against on-chain nullifiers — and future impersonation is possible. The system provides session secrecy (a stolen session ID is useless after 24 hours) but not key secrecy (a stolen private key is useful indefinitely). The system provides no mechanism for key rotation without creating a new commitment; commitment revocation is not currently implemented.

Single-party trusted setup. The circuit-specific setup phase was performed with a single contributor. A multi-party ceremony would reduce the trust assumption to the weakest honest participant.

Prototype key storage weakness. The current implementation derives the AES encryption key for sk from the public wallet address rather than from a wallet signature. This means any party with access to the user’s localStorage and knowledge of their wallet address can decrypt the stored secret key. A production deployment must derive the key from a wallet signature via HKDF-SHA-256, ensuring only the wallet holder can decrypt.

No Sybil resistance. Any address can register a commitment. The system does not prevent a single individual from creating multiple ZK identities with different wallets.

Admin and verifier upgrade trust. The authVerifier address in ZKAuthRegistry is upgradeable by the admin via setVerifier(), and all four contracts support UUPS logic upgrades restricted to ADMIN_ROLE. A compromised admin key can therefore swap the verifier for a trivial one — causing all proof submissions to pass — or replace contract logic entirely. In the current prototype the admin is a single externally owned account. A production deployment should replace this with a multi-signature wallet and a timelock controller so that upgrade proposals are publicly visible before taking effect.

Batch issuance cross-contract overhead. As noted in Section V-C, batch certificate issuance performs one cross-contract call per certificate, which is gas-inefficient for large batches.

VIII. Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presented zkCredentials, a privacy-preserving educational credential management and ZK-authenticated access system deployed on zkSync Era. The system demonstrates that Groth16 ZKPs combined with a Poseidon-based commitment and nullifier scheme can provide practical, computationally hiding authentication on a production EVM-compatible Layer-2 network. Proof generation completes in 2.1–7.8 s across representative hardware, and on-chain ZK verification consumes approximately 210,000 gas on zkSync Sepolia. The predecessor UltraPlonk implementation could not be deployed to testnet due to zksolc incompatibility, making Groth16 the viable choice for this target. The key engineering contribution is the identification and resolution of the forceEVMLA compilation incompatibility that blocked Groth16 verifier deployment on zkSync Era.

Privacy guarantees are explicitly bounded: the system provides computational hiding of credential contents and session nullifier unlinkability, but does not hide the authenticating wallet address from a passive observer. This scope is appropriate for a credential verification use case where selective disclosure (not full anonymity) is the primary requirement.

A. Future Work

  • Relayer integration or Merkle anonymity set to achieve wallet-level unlinkability during authentication.
  • Selective credential disclosure via an extended circuit that proves specific attributes (e.g., graduation year, degree level) without revealing the full credential.
  • Key rotation via a circuit proving knowledge of both the old and new secret key, allowing forward-secure key migration.
  • Multi-party trusted setup ceremony for the circuit-specific phase, distributing trust among independent contributors.
  • PLONK [21] or Halo2 [28] migration to eliminate per-circuit trusted setup requirements, at some cost to proof size and verification gas.
  • Issuer-side ZK proofs allowing institutions to prove off-chain facts about certificates without storing them on-chain, extending privacy protections to the issuance flow.
  • Batch count optimisation to replace N cross-contract calls in batch issuance with a single aggregated increment.
