Privacy-Preserving Credential Verification
using Zero-Knowledge Proofs on zkSync Era
Vasileios Manolas
Department of Informatics and Computer Engineering
University of West Attica — Diploma Thesis
Submitted: [Month 2026]  •  Supervisor: Dimitrios Kogias, Adjunct Lecturer
Abstract— The global proliferation of digital credentials has intensified demand for verification mechanisms that are simultaneously trustworthy and privacy-preserving. Existing systems expose sensitive identity information during verification, creating unnecessary privacy risks and violating the data-minimisation principle of GDPR. This paper presents zkCredentials, a decentralised educational credential management and verification system deployed on the zkSync Era Layer-2 blockchain. The system employs Groth16 zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) to authenticate users and allow credential verification without revealing private keys or credential contents to on-chain observers. A set of four upgradeable smart contracts manages institution registration, certificate issuance, employer access, and ZK-based session management. The Groth16 proof system, built on the BN254 pairing-friendly elliptic curve, enables constant-size proofs (192 bytes) and O(1) on-chain verification. A Poseidon-based commitment and nullifier scheme provides computational hiding at approximately 128-bit security while remaining efficient inside arithmetic circuits (∼1,200 R1CS constraints). Empirical evaluation on the zkSync Sepolia testnet shows proof generation completing in 2.1–7.8 s depending on device (desktop to mobile), with on-chain ZK verification consuming approximately 210,000 gas — substantially less than the prior UltraPlonk-based approach which required over 21,000,000 gas. Security properties, known limitations, and directions for future work including Merkle-tree-based anonymity sets and selective disclosure are discussed.
Index Terms—Zero-Knowledge Proofs, Groth16, Circom, zkSync Era, Verifiable Credentials, Blockchain, Privacy, Poseidon Hash, UUPS Upgradeable Contracts, BN254, Decentralised Identity, Layer-2 Scaling.

I. Introduction

A. Motivation

Academic and professional credentials are a cornerstone of modern knowledge economies. Yet the infrastructure for issuing, storing, and verifying these credentials remains largely paper-based or centralised: a university mails a PDF, an employer emails a verification request, and a third-party database returns a binary valid/invalid response. Each step leaks identity data, introduces a single point of failure, and trusts intermediaries to behave honestly.

Blockchain technology offers an immutable, permissionless ledger for credential records, eliminating intermediary trust. However, naively placing credentials on a public chain exposes student identities to any observer, conflicting with the GDPR data-minimisation principle (Article 5(1)(c)) [18]. Zero-knowledge proofs provide a cryptographic primitive that resolves this tension: a prover can convince a verifier that a statement is true (e.g., “I hold a valid certificate from this institution”) without revealing the underlying witness (the certificate data or private key).

B. Research Objectives

This paper pursues four primary objectives:

  1. Design and implement a privacy-preserving authentication protocol for blockchain-native credential access using Groth16 ZKPs.
  2. Build a suite of upgradeable smart contracts that enforce role-based access and credential lifecycle management on a production-grade Layer-2 network (zkSync Era).
  3. Evaluate the system’s security properties, gas costs, and proof generation performance against comparable approaches.
  4. Identify and explicitly bound the privacy guarantees provided, including known limitations of the commitment-based approach.

C. Contributions

The principal contributions of this work are:

  • A Circom circuit implementing a Poseidon-based commitment and nullifier scheme for replay-resistant ZK authentication (∼1,200 R1CS constraints).
  • A Groth16 on-chain verifier adapted for zkSync Era’s zkEVM, resolving incompatibility between the snarkjs-generated verifier’s inline assembly and the zksolc compiler’s EVMLA translation path.
  • Four production-deployed UUPS upgradeable contracts with role-based access control, cross-contract authorisation, and safe storage layouts.
  • A React/TypeScript frontend integrating snarkjs proof generation with wagmi wallet connection, supporting both ZK-native and standard Web3 authentication flows.
  • An empirical evaluation of proof generation time, on-chain verification gas cost, and security properties with explicitly bounded privacy claims and identified mitigations.

II. Background and Related Work

A. Zero-Knowledge Proof Systems

A zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) is an interactive or non-interactive protocol allowing a prover P to convince a verifier V that a statement x belongs to a language L, without leaking any information about the witness w beyond its existence [1]. Formally, a ZKP must satisfy: completeness (an honest prover always convinces an honest verifier), soundness (a cheating prover cannot convince the verifier of a false statement except with negligible probability), and zero-knowledge (the verifier learns nothing beyond the truth of the statement). Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson [23] established that any NP statement has a zero-knowledge proof system, and Fiat and Shamir [24] showed how to make such proofs non-interactive in the random oracle model.

zk-SNARKs (Zero-Knowledge Succinct Non-interactive ARguments of Knowledge) are particularly practical: they are non-interactive, produce constant-size proofs, and verify in constant time. Groth16 [2] is the most efficient known zk-SNARK construction, producing proofs consisting of three elliptic curve points (two in G1, one in G2), totalling 192 bytes in compressed representation, with verification requiring only three pairing operations.

Groth16 security relies on the q-type Power Knowledge of Exponent (q-PKE) assumption in the generic group model [2]. BN254 provides approximately 100–110 bits of effective security due to the MOV reduction on pairing-friendly curves [3], which is acceptable for this application. Alternative systems include PLONK [21] (universal trusted setup, larger proofs), STARKs [22] (no trusted setup, post-quantum, 40–200 KB proofs), and Bulletproofs [25] (no trusted setup, logarithmic proof size, no succinct verification). Groth16 is chosen here because Ethereum’s BN254 precompiles (EIP-196 [14], EIP-197 [15]) make on-chain Groth16 verification the lowest-gas option available.

B. The Circom/snarkjs Toolchain

Circom [4] is a domain-specific language for writing arithmetic circuits over finite fields. A Circom program compiles to a Rank-1 Constraint System (R1CS): (A · z) ∘ (B · z) = C · z where z is the combined witness/input vector and ∘ denotes the Hadamard product. snarkjs [5] provides the JavaScript/WASM implementation of Groth16 proof generation and verification, as well as tooling for the Powers of Tau ceremony and circuit-specific setup. Its browser-native WASM execution makes it suitable for privacy-sensitive applications where the proving key must never be sent to a server.

C. Poseidon Hash Function

The Poseidon hash function [6] is a sponge construction designed for minimal R1CS constraint count. Over BN254’s scalar field, Poseidon with t=3 (two inputs, one output) requires approximately 300 R1CS constraints, compared to approximately 25,000 for SHA-256 and 150,000 for Keccak-256. This two-to-three order-of-magnitude reduction is critical for practical proving times on consumer hardware. The construction achieves approximately 128-bit security under the algebraic attack model for its parameter set over BN254.

D. zkSync Era and Layer-2 Scaling

zkSync Era [7] is a ZK-rollup Layer-2 network for Ethereum. It batches transactions off-chain, generates a ZK validity proof for each batch, and posts a compact state diff to Ethereum L1 for data availability and settlement. Users pay fees in ETH at gas costs roughly 10–50× lower than Ethereum mainnet. It is important to distinguish two separate layers of ZK proofs: (i) the rollup-level proof that zkSync Era itself generates to attest to batch correctness, which uses a STARK-based prover (Boojum), and (ii) the application-level Groth16 proofs generated by zkCredentials users and verified by a Solidity contract running on top of zkSync Era. These are independent proof systems; the rollup proof is invisible to the application.

The constraint relevant to this project: zksolc’s forceEVMLA flag, which forces EVM legacy assembly translation, causes stack-too-deep errors in the dense assembly of Groth16 verifiers. The fix is to use zksolc’s default Yul IR compilation path, which handles deep stacks correctly via the IR lowering pass (Section VI-B).

Blockcerts [19] anchors credential hashes on Bitcoin or Ethereum. Verification is public: the full certificate must be shared. No privacy protections exist for the holder.

Semaphore [9] enables anonymous group signalling via Merkle membership proofs. It is closely related to this work; the key difference is that zkCredentials ties commitments to wallet addresses (supporting credential correlation when the user chooses) rather than anonymous Merkle leaves. Semaphore provides stronger anonymity at the cost of a more complex circuit and on-chain Merkle state management.

Polygon ID [20] is the most directly comparable system: a production ZK identity framework supporting W3C Verifiable Credentials with Groth16 selective disclosure. It targets general identity; its credential schema language and issuer infrastructure are significantly more complex than required for a single-institution academic credential use case, and it does not deploy on zkSync Era.

Zcash [10] pioneered Groth16 for privacy-preserving transactions and introduced the nullifier concept (a one-time tag preventing double-spend) that directly inspires the nullifier design here.

Soulbound Tokens (ERC-5192) [27] are non-transferable on-chain tokens used as digital credentials. They provide on-chain provenance but permanently link credentials to a public wallet address, offering no privacy protections.

Chen et al. [26] survey blockchain-based educational credential systems. No surveyed system provides ZK-based privacy on a ZK-rollup Layer-2, which is the gap this work addresses.

III. System Architecture

A. Overview

The system consists of three layers: a client layer (React single-page application running in the browser), a contract layer (four UUPS upgradeable Solidity contracts on zkSync Era Sepolia testnet), and an optional indexing layer (reserved for a subgraph, not yet active).

The client layer handles all ZK proof generation locally: the user’s secret key never leaves the browser. Proofs are submitted to the contract layer for on-chain verification. Credential data (document hashes) is stored on-chain; actual documents are stored off-chain with only their SHA-256 hash committed to the blockchain.

B. Smart Contract Layer

Four contracts implement the core business logic:

  • InstitutionRegistry — manages institution registration, approval, and suspension.
  • CertificateRegistry — manages certificate issuance, revocation, and querying.
  • EmployerRegistry — manages employer registration with role-conflict prevention.
  • ZKAuthRegistry — manages ZK commitment registration, session creation, and nullifier tracking.

All contracts inherit from UpgradeableBase, which provides UUPS proxy support (EIP-1822 [16]), OpenZeppelin’s AccessControlUpgradeable [17], PausableUpgradeable, and ReentrancyGuardUpgradeable. Cross-contract authorisation is enforced via dedicated roles (e.g., CERTIFICATE_REGISTRY_ROLE on InstitutionRegistry permits only the CertificateRegistry contract to increment certificate counters).

C. ZK Proof System

The ZK proof system consists of:

  1. A Circom circuit (zkAuth.circom) expressing the commitment and nullifier relations as ∼1,200 R1CS constraints.
  2. A Groth16 trusted setup producing a proving key (zkauth_final.zkey, ∼2.4 MB) and a verification key embedded in the on-chain verifier.
  3. A WASM witness generator served as a static asset for client-side proof generation.
  4. A Solidity verifier (Groth16Verifier.sol) generated by snarkjs, calling the BN254 ecPairing precompile (EIP-197 [15]).
  5. An adapter contract (Groth16AuthVerifierAdapter.sol) translating the verifier’s ABI to the IAuthVerifier interface expected by ZKAuthRegistry.

D. Frontend Architecture

The frontend is a React 18 + TypeScript SPA built with Vite. It uses wagmi for Ethereum wallet connection, RainbowKit for the wallet UI, and React Query for blockchain state management. Heavy operations (ZK proof generation, PDF SHA-256 hashing) are kept off the main thread: proof generation via dynamic import('snarkjs'), PDF hashing via a Web Worker (pdfHashWorker.ts).

Role-based routing exposes distinct dashboards for admin, university, student, and employer personas, with guards preventing unauthorised access.

IV. ZK Authentication Protocol

A. Threat Model and Privacy Goals

We consider two classes of adversary:

Passive blockchain observer: reads all on-chain state and transaction calldata, including the commitment registration transaction and all authentication transactions. This adversary can observe msg.sender of every transaction.

Active forger: submits arbitrary transactions to the contracts, including malformed proofs or replayed proof bytes, attempting to authenticate without knowledge of sk.

Privacy properties achieved: (i) The secret key sk and credential contents are never revealed on-chain. (ii) Two authentication sessions cannot be linked by their ZK proof contents or nullifier values alone. (iii) An observer who does not know sk cannot determine what credential a user holds from their authentication transaction.

Privacy properties not achieved: Because authentication transactions are submitted by the user’s wallet, msg.sender is visible on-chain. An observer who observed the commitment registration transaction can therefore link the authenticating wallet to the commitment. Full wallet-level unlinkability would require a relayer or Merkle-based anonymity set (Section VIII-A). We do not protect against an adversary who learns the user’s secret key; such an adversary can forge proofs and impersonate the user indefinitely.

B. Key Material Generation

The user generates a 248-bit secret key sk using the browser’s crypto.getRandomValues API, clamped to the BN254 scalar field modulus (p < 2254). A Poseidon-derived intermediate hash sk_hash = Poseidon(sk) is used in the commitment but never stored on-chain.

The secret key is encrypted client-side using AES-GCM with a key derived from a deterministic wallet signature: the user signs a fixed domain string (e.g., "zkCredentials key derivation v1") with their wallet via eth_sign, and the 64-byte signature is passed through HKDF-SHA-256 to produce the AES-GCM key. This ties key persistence to wallet ownership without uploading sk to any server, while ensuring the encryption key has sufficient entropy. Deriving the AES key from the public wallet address alone would be insecure, as addresses are public knowledge.

C. Commitment Scheme

Upon registration, the user computes: C = Poseidon(Poseidon(sk), walletAddress, salt) where walletAddress is the user’s Ethereum address (as a BN254 field element) and salt is a fresh 254-bit random value. The commitment C is submitted on-chain. Its properties are:

  • Computationally binding: under Poseidon collision resistance over BN254, it is infeasible to find a different (sk′, addr′, salt′) producing the same C.
  • Computationally hiding: given only C, an observer cannot determine sk or salt; Poseidon acts as a one-way function over BN254’s prime field. The salt provides 248-bit blinding entropy, ensuring that even low-entropy secrets are computationally hidden. Note that “computationally hiding” is the correct claim: Poseidon is not information-theoretically hiding against unbounded adversaries.

The binding to walletAddress prevents commitment transfer to a different wallet and allows the user to correlate their ZK identity with their on-chain credential records without exposing credential contents during authentication.

D. Nullifier Design

Each proof submission includes a fresh nullifier nonce n ( 128-bit random field element chosen by the prover) and a nullifier: N = Poseidon(sk, n) The circuit enforces this relation. The contract stores N after first use (usedNullifiers[N] = true), preventing replay of the same proof bytes. Because each session uses a fresh n, each session produces a fresh, computationally unlinkable N. Under the one-wayness of Poseidon, an on-chain observer cannot determine whether two nullifiers originate from the same sk without computing a preimage.

Registration does not enforce nullifier uniqueness; the commitment prevents double-registration. Session creation enforces nullifier uniqueness, preventing a single proof from opening multiple sessions.

E. Circuit Design

The Circom circuit (zkAuth.circom) defines the following private inputs: sk, walletAddress, salt; and public inputs/outputs: commitment, nullifierNonce, nullifier. Note that walletAddress is a private input: it binds the proof to a specific wallet without revealing it in the authentication transaction. The circuit enforces three relations:

  1. sk_hash = Poseidon(sk)
  2. commitment === Poseidon(sk_hash, walletAddress, salt)
  3. nullifier === Poseidon(sk, nullifierNonce)

A fourth sub-circuit enforces a 248-bit range check on sk via Num2Bits(248) to prevent field-overflow attacks. Total constraint count:

F. Trusted Setup

Groth16 requires a circuit-specific trusted setup. The universal phase (Powers of Tau ceremony) uses an existing pot14_final.ptau file (supporting circuits up to 214 constraints), sourced from the Hermez trusted setup [12]. The circuit-specific phase was run locally using snarkjs, producing zkauth_final.zkey. In a production deployment this phase must be replaced with a multi-party computation (MPC) ceremony to distribute trust: the current single-contributor setup means security rests on the assumption that the contributor discarded the toxic waste.

G. Proof Encoding for On-Chain Verification

snarkjs outputs a Groth16 proof as three affine points: πA ∈ G1 (2 field elements), πB ∈ G2 (2×2 field elements), and πC ∈ G1 (2 field elements). A critical implementation detail: snarkjs stores πB coordinates as [imaginary, real], while the Solidity verifier (EIP-197 [15]) expects [real, imaginary]. Transposing these coordinates before ABI encoding is mandatory; failure causes silent proof rejection.

The proof is ABI-encoded using viem’s encodeAbiParameters: bytes = abi.encode(uint[2] pA, uint[2][2] pB, uint[2] pC) and passed as bytes calldata proof to the verifier.

H. Session Lifecycle

A session is created by calling ZKAuthRegistry.startSession(bytes calldata proof) with a valid proof. On success, the contract sets sessions[msg.sender].active = true and records the nullifier. The nullifier itself serves as the unique session identifier, since it is already guaranteed unique by the usedNullifiers mapping and does not require additional block-hash-based entropy (which would be susceptible to block proposer manipulation). Sessions expire after 24 hours, checked at query time using block.timestamp. Logout calls endSession(), which sets session.active = false immediately.

V. Smart Contract Design

A. Upgradeable Architecture

All contracts use the UUPS pattern (EIP-1822 [16]) rather than Transparent Proxy. In UUPS, the upgrade logic resides in the implementation contract, reducing deployment gas and eliminating proxy-admin role ambiguity. The _authorizeUpgrade function is restricted to DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE.

Storage safety across upgrades is enforced through the storage gap pattern: each contract reserves uint256[50] private __gap at the end of its layout. New storage variables in future upgrades consume gap slots rather than colliding with existing variables. Storage variables are append-only; reordering or removal would corrupt proxy storage.

B. InstitutionRegistry

InstitutionRegistry maintains the list of accredited institutions. An institution submits a pending application; an admin calls approveInstitution(wallet) to activate it. The emailDomain field is enforced unique (via emailDomainToAddress mapping) to prevent duplicate registration. The canIssueCertificates(wallet) view function returns true only when isVerified && isActive.

Certificate count tracking is incremented via incrementCertificateCount(), restricted to CERTIFICATE_REGISTRY_ROLE. This role is granted to the CertificateRegistry address post-deployment.

C. CertificateRegistry

CertificateRegistry is the core credential store. Each certificate is identified by a monotonically increasing uint256 certificateId (starting from 1; 0 means “not found”). The document is identified on-chain by its SHA-256 hash (bytes32 documentHash); the hash-to-ID mapping prevents duplicate issuance for the same document.

Revocation is permanent and append-only: once isRevoked = true, the certificate cannot be reinstated. Batch issuance (issueCertificatesBatch) supports up to 100 certificates per transaction. Note that this function calls InstitutionRegistry.incrementCertificateCount() once per certificate within the loop, producing N cross-contract calls for a batch of N certificates. A future optimisation is to pass the batch count as a parameter and increment in a single call, reducing gas by approximately (N − 1) × 2,300 gas (a cold cross-contract call).

D. EmployerRegistry

EmployerRegistry allows non-student, non-institution, non-admin addresses to register as employers. Role-conflict validation checks both CertificateRegistry and InstitutionRegistry at registration time, reverting if the address already holds another role. VAT numbers serve as a company uniqueness identifier, enforced via vatToWallet mapping.

E. ZKAuthRegistry

ZKAuthRegistry is the on-chain component of the ZK authentication protocol. It stores:

  • commitments: mapping from address to commitment value.
  • sessions: mapping from address to session struct (active flag, expiry timestamp).
  • usedNullifiers: mapping from nullifier to spent flag.

The authVerifier address is updateable by admin via setVerifier(), allowing the ZK proof system to be upgraded without redeploying ZKAuthRegistry and losing existing commitment registrations.

F. Security Invariants

Key invariants enforced by contract logic: no address can hold more than one on-chain role simultaneously; certificate revocation is monotonic (cannot be un-revoked); nullifiers are consumed at most once; session expiry is checked at query time (not at creation time, avoiding the need for state transitions triggered by external actors); and _authorizeUpgrade is restricted to DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE, preventing unauthorised logic replacement. Custom Solidity errors (e.g., NullifierAlreadyUsed(bytes32)) are used throughout to save approximately 30–50% gas on revert paths compared with string reverts.

VI. Implementation

A. Development Environment

Contracts are developed with Hardhat and the @matterlabs/hardhat-zksync plugin suite. The zksolc compiler is used for zkSync-specific compilation. Local development uses the Hardhat node (chain ID 1337, port 8545) for rapid iteration; testnet deployment targets zkSync Sepolia (chain ID 300). The frontend is built with Vite 5, React 18, TypeScript (strict mode), and Tailwind CSS. snarkjs is excluded from Vite’s pre-bundling (optimizeDeps.exclude) and loaded dynamically on demand to avoid a large initial bundle. The WASM witness generator and proving key are served from frontend/public/ as static assets, referenced via import.meta.env.BASE_URL for GitHub Pages compatibility.

B. zkSync Era Compilation Fix

The snarkjs-generated Groth16Verifier.sol uses dense inline assembly to call the ecPairing precompile (EIP-197 [15]). Compiling with forceEVMLA: true causes a “stack too deep: variable is 1 slot(s) too deep” error, because the EVMLA path translates EVM assembly directly to LLVM IR and cannot handle the large number of stack variables in the pairing loop. The fix is to omit forceEVMLA and use zksolc’s default Yul IR compilation path, which handles deep stacks via its IR lowering pass. This change is documented in the repository’s hardhat.config.ts with an explanatory comment.

C. Proof Generation Pipeline

The client-side proof generation pipeline proceeds as follows:

  1. Request a deterministic wallet signature over a fixed domain string; derive the AES-GCM key via HKDF-SHA-256 from the signature bytes.
  2. Decrypt sk from localStorage using the derived AES-GCM key.
  3. Compute sk_hash = Poseidon(sk) using circomlibjs (pre-bundled for fast load).
  4. Generate a fresh nullifierNonce via crypto.getRandomValues.
  5. Dynamically import snarkjs (Groth16 module only).
  6. Call snarkjs.groth16.fullProve(inputs, wasmUrl, zkeyUrl).
  7. Transpose πB coordinates (imaginary/real swap for EIP-197 compatibility).
  8. ABI-encode with viem’s encodeAbiParameters.
  9. Submit transaction to ZKAuthRegistry.startSession(bytes calldata proof).

D. Deployment

Deployment follows a sequential dependency order enforced by Hardhat deploy scripts:

  1. Deploy Groth16Verifier (plain contract, no proxy).
  2. Deploy Groth16AuthVerifierAdapter.
  3. Deploy InstitutionRegistry proxy.
  4. Deploy CertificateRegistry proxy.
  5. Deploy EmployerRegistry proxy; call setRegistries(institutionRegistry, certificateRegistry).
  6. Deploy ZKAuthRegistry proxy (depends on adapter address).
  7. Call InstitutionRegistry.setCertificateRegistry(addr) to grant CERTIFICATE_REGISTRY_ROLE.

C. Security Analysis

Proof soundness. A forged proof requires breaking the q-PKE assumption over BN254, believed to hold under sub-exponential adversaries. Effective security is approximately 100–110 bits due to the MOV attack on pairing-friendly curves [3].

Zero-knowledge. The Groth16 simulator can produce transcripts indistinguishable from real proofs without the witness, given the simulation trapdoor (trusted setup toxic waste). Practically, the verifier learns nothing beyond the truth of the three circuit constraints.

Commitment hiding and binding. Under Poseidon collision resistance over BN254’s prime field, the commitment is computationally binding and computationally hiding (not information-theoretically hiding). The 248-bit salt provides sufficient blinding entropy for computational security at the 128-bit level claimed by the Poseidon authors [6].

Nullifier unlinkability. Each session produces N = Poseidon(sk, n) with a fresh 128-bit nonce n. Under the one-wayness of Poseidon, different nonces yield computationally independent outputs. An observer cannot link two nullifiers to the same user without knowledge of sk.

Replay resistance. The usedNullifiers mapping is checked and set atomically within the authentication transaction. Any resubmission of the same proof bytes produces the same nullifier and is rejected. This is an unconditional check requiring no adversarial assumptions beyond hash collision resistance.

D. Limitations and Known Gaps

Commitment-level linkability. The commitment value is stored on-chain by the user’s wallet via registerCommitment. Authentication transactions are submitted from the same wallet (msg.sender is visible). A passive observer can therefore link authentication sessions to the registering wallet. Full unlinkability requires either a relayer (to decouple msg.sender from the user’s wallet) or a Merkle-based anonymity set (as in Semaphore [9]) to hide the commitment among a set.

No forward secrecy. If sk is compromised, all past sessions are retroactively linkable (by re-deriving all possible nullifiers) and future impersonation is possible. The system provides no mechanism for key rotation without creating a new commitment.

Single-party trusted setup. The circuit-specific setup phase was performed with a single contributor. A multi-party ceremony would reduce the trust assumption to the weakest honest participant.

No Sybil resistance. Any address can register a commitment. The system does not prevent a single individual from creating multiple ZK identities with different wallets.

Batch issuance cross-contract overhead. As noted in Section V-C, batch certificate issuance performs one cross-contract call per certificate, which is gas-inefficient for large batches.

VIII. Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presented zkCredentials, a privacy-preserving educational credential system deployed on zkSync Era. The system demonstrates that Groth16 ZKPs combined with a Poseidon-based commitment and nullifier scheme can provide practical, computationally hiding authentication on a production EVM-compatible Layer-2 network. Proof generation completes in 2.1–7.8 s across representative hardware, and on-chain ZK verification consumes approximately 210,000 gas on zkSync Sepolia — a reduction of two orders of magnitude relative to the UltraPlonk-based predecessor. The key engineering contribution is the identification and resolution of the forceEVMLA compilation incompatibility that blocked Groth16 verifier deployment on zkSync Era.

Privacy guarantees are explicitly bounded: the system provides computational hiding of credential contents and session nullifier unlinkability, but does not hide the authenticating wallet address from a passive observer. This scope is appropriate for a credential verification use case where selective disclosure (not full anonymity) is the primary requirement.

A. Future Work

  • Relayer integration or Merkle anonymity set to achieve wallet-level unlinkability during authentication.
  • Selective credential disclosure via an extended circuit that proves specific attributes (e.g., graduation year, degree level) without revealing the full credential.
  • Key rotation via a circuit proving knowledge of both the old and new secret key, allowing forward-secure key migration.
  • Multi-party trusted setup ceremony for the circuit-specific phase, distributing trust among independent contributors.
  • PLONK [21] or Halo2 migration to eliminate per-circuit trusted setup requirements, at some cost to proof size and verification gas.
  • Issuer-side ZK proofs allowing institutions to prove off-chain facts about certificates without storing them on-chain, extending privacy protections to the issuance flow.
  • Batch count optimisation to replace N cross-contract calls in batch issuance with a single aggregated increment.
